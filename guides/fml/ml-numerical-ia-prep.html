01 Simple Linear Regression — Least Squares Method

Theory

We're fitting a straight line $\hat{y} = mx + c$ through a set of $(x_i, y_i)$ points, choosing $m$ (slope) and $c$ (intercept) to minimize the squared error between actual $y$ and predicted $\hat y$.

The closed-form (calculus-derived) formulas are:

$$ m = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar x)^2} \qquad\qquad c = \bar y - m\bar x $$

where $\bar x$ and $\bar y$ are the means of $x$ and $y$.

Error / goodness-of-fit metrics — these almost always follow the slope/intercept calculation in an exam question:

$$ SS_{tot} = \sum (y_i - \bar y)^2 \qquad SS_{res} = \sum (y_i - \hat y_i)^2 $$ $$ MSE = \frac{SS_{res}}{n} \qquad RMSE = \sqrt{MSE} \qquad R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{res}}{SS_{tot}} $$

$R^2$ tells you what fraction of the variation in $y$ is "explained" by $x$ — closer to 1 is a better fit.

02 Multiple Linear Regression — Normal Equation

Theory

With more than one input feature, we fit $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \dots + \beta_p x_p$. The closed-form solution (no iteration needed) is the Normal Equation:

$$ \hat\beta = (X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty $$

where $X$ is the design matrix — every row is one data point, with a leading column of 1's for the intercept $\beta_0$.

The five calculation steps every time:

  1. Build $X$ (add the column of 1's) and the target vector $y$.
  2. Compute $X^T$ (transpose).
  3. Compute the product $X^TX$ (a square matrix).
  4. Invert $X^TX$ to get $(X^TX)^{-1}$.
  5. Multiply: $\hat\beta = (X^TX)^{-1}(X^Ty)$.

03 Linear Regression via Gradient Descent (SGD)

Theory

Instead of solving for coefficients in closed form, Stochastic Gradient Descent starts with $B_0=0, B_1=0$ and updates them one training example at a time, nudging them a little closer to the optimum on each pass:

$$ p(i) = B_0 + B_1\cdot x(i) \qquad \text{error} = p(i)-y(i) $$ $$ B_0^{(t+1)} = B_0^{(t)} - \alpha \cdot \text{error} \qquad B_1^{(t+1)} = B_1^{(t)} - \alpha \cdot \text{error}\cdot x(i) $$

$\alpha$ is the learning rate (a small constant like 0.01 or 0.1). One full pass through all training points is called an epoch.

Exam tip: the question will almost always give you a small dataset (4–5 points), an initial $B_0, B_1$ (usually both 0), a value of $\alpha$, and ask you to hand-compute 1–2 iterations or one full epoch, showing $p(i)$, error, and the updated $B_0, B_1$ at every step.

04 Logistic Regression — Sigmoid, Odds & Predictions

Theory

Logistic regression is a classification method (despite the name). It squashes a linear combination $z = B_0 + B_1x$ through the sigmoid function into a probability between 0 and 1:

$$ P(x) = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} = \frac{e^z}{1+e^z} $$

Odds and log-odds (logit) — the ratio of "event happens" to "event doesn't happen":

$$ \text{odds} = \frac{P}{1-P} \qquad \text{logit}(P) = \ln\left(\frac{P}{1-P}\right) = z = B_0+B_1x $$

Decision rule: predict class $1$ if $P \geq 0.5$, else predict class $0$.

Worked Example 1 — Predicting probability from coefficients

Given $B_0=-100,\ B_1=0.6$. Find $P(\text{rich}\mid \text{income}=150)$.

Step 1 — Compute $z$ $z = -100 + 0.6(150) = -100+90 = -10$
Step 2 — Apply sigmoid $$P = \frac{1}{1+e^{-(-10)}} = \frac{1}{1+e^{10}} = \frac{1}{1+22026.47} = 0.0000453979$$
Step 3 — Decision Since $0.0000454 < 0.5$, predict class $0$ (poor).

Note: your slide rounds/prints this as "0.000453978687" — the precise value is $4.5398\times10^{-5} = 0.0000453979$; same digits, watch the decimal placement if you're copying it into an exam answer.

P ≈ 0.0000454 → predict "poor" (class 0)
Worked Example 2 — Odds and log-odds from a probability

If $P(\text{win}) = 0.6$:

$$\text{odds} = \frac{0.6}{1-0.6} = \frac{0.6}{0.4} = 1.5 \qquad \text{logit}(P) = \ln(1.5) = 0.405$$ odds = 1.5, log-odds = 0.405

06 How to Approach the Numerical Question in the Exam

Strategy
  1. Identify the method first. Look for the giveaways: a design matrix / matrix inverse notation → Normal Equation. Words like "iteration," "epoch," "learning rate α" → Gradient Descent. A single $(x,y)$ pair with slope/intercept only → Simple Linear Regression. Anything about "probability," "sigmoid," or "classify" → Logistic Regression.
  2. Write the general formula first, then substitute numbers — examiners give partial credit for the correct formula even if arithmetic slips.
  3. Show intermediate sums/matrices explicitly (like the $\bar x, \bar y$ table or the $X^TX$ matrix above) rather than jumping straight to the final number — this is exactly the style your slides use, and it's what graders expect to see.
  4. Box or underline your final answer (coefficients, $R^2$, or probability).
  5. Sanity-check: plug your fitted coefficients back into one data point and confirm the prediction is reasonably close to the actual $y$ — this catches arithmetic errors before you hand in the paper.
