Engineering Economics & Financial Management

A complete worked guide to cash flow diagrams, the seven compound interest formulas, nominal/effective interest, and comparing alternatives by present and future worth — every numerical from your lecture decks, solved and explained.

How to use this guide

Everything in this subject's numerical portion reduces to one question: "I have money at time A, I want its equivalent at time B — which factor moves it?" There are only seven factors. Section 1 teaches you to draw the picture; Section 2 gives you the seven tools with every problem from your slides worked line by line; Section 3 shows what happens when one tool isn't enough; Section 4 adds the one remaining wrinkle — rates that don't compound the way they're stated; Sections 5, 6, and 7 turn the same toolkit on comparing alternatives — present worth, future worth, then annual worth; Section 8 is your night-before revision sheet.

Section 0Interest Foundations

Before cash flow diagrams make sense, you need the idea they exist to serve: money has a time value. A rupee today is not the same object as a rupee next year, because a rupee today can be lent out and will come back as more than a rupee. Two forces drive this:

  • Earning power. Money put to productive use generates more money. Giving up the use of your money for a year is a real sacrifice, and interest is the rent you charge for it.
  • Purchasing power. Inflation erodes what a rupee buys. Even a lender who wanted zero profit would need compensation just to break even in real terms.

Interest is therefore not an arbitrary fee. Your deck lists three things a lender is being compensated for: the lost earnings from money that could have been invested productively, the risk that the loan is not repaid, and the administrative expense of making the loan.

The standard symbols — memorise these
  • P — present sum, a single amount at time 0 (also called principal or present worth)
  • F — future sum, a single amount at the end of period \(n\)
  • A — a uniform amount occurring at the end of every period, from period 1 through \(n\)
  • G — a uniform gradient, the constant amount by which a series increases each period
  • i — interest rate per interest period (always as a decimal in formulas: 15% → 0.15)
  • n — number of interest periods

Simple interest

Under simple interest, interest is charged only on the original principal. Interest already earned does not itself earn interest — it sits inert. So the interest accrued is directly proportional to the principal, the rate, and the time:

$$I = P \cdot i \cdot N \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad F = P + I = P(1 + iN)$$

Notice this is linear in \(N\). The balance grows along a straight line. This is the exception in engineering economics, not the rule — real financial instruments almost always compound — but it appears in your assessment as a warm-up.

Simple Interest · Q1

Simple interest on a certain sum is Rs. 4016.25 at 9% per annum in 5 years. Find the sum.

What is given, what is asked. We know the interest earned \(I\), not the final amount. We want \(P\). This is a direct substitution into \(I = PiN\) — the only reason it feels like a puzzle is that you are solving for the quantity inside the product rather than the product itself.

$$4016.25 = P \times 0.09 \times 5 = 0.45P \;\Longrightarrow\; P = \frac{4016.25}{0.45}$$

Answer

\(P = \) Rs. 8,925

Simple Interest · Q2

A sum of Rs. 25,000 will become Rs. 31,000 in 48 months at some rate of simple interest. Find the rate of interest per annum.

The trap here is units. The rate is asked "per annum" but the duration is given in months. 48 months = 4 years, and \(N\) must be measured in the same period as \(i\). Convert first, always.

$$F = P(1+iN) \;\Longrightarrow\; 31000 = 25000(1 + 4i)$$

$$\frac{31000}{25000} = 1.24 = 1 + 4i \;\Longrightarrow\; 4i = 0.24 \;\Longrightarrow\; i = 0.06$$

Answer

\(i = \) 6% per annum

Simple Interest · Q3

A sum of Rs. 12,000 is lent out at 5% per annum simple interest for 5 years. What will be the amount after 5 years?

Direct forward substitution — nothing hidden.

$$F = P(1+iN) = 12000\,(1 + 0.05 \times 5) = 12000 \times 1.25$$

Answer

\(F = \) Rs. 15,000 (of which Rs. 3,000 is interest)

Simple Interest · Q4

A person deposited some amount in a bank at simple interest. After 15 years the amount became seven times. In how many years will the amount become ten times, if the rate stays the same?

Why this one is worth understanding properly. No principal and no rate are given, and none are needed — because under simple interest the multiple of the principal depends only on the product \(iN\). This is exactly the property that fails under compounding, so the technique here is specific to simple interest.

The amount becoming seven times means \(F = 7P\):

$$7P = P(1 + i \cdot 15) \;\Longrightarrow\; 7 = 1 + 15i \;\Longrightarrow\; 15i = 6 \;\Longrightarrow\; i = 0.4 \text{ (i.e. 40\% p.a.)}$$

Now impose the ten-times condition with that same \(i\):

$$10P = P(1 + 0.4N) \;\Longrightarrow\; 9 = 0.4N \;\Longrightarrow\; N = 22.5$$

Answer

22.5 years

Shortcut worth memorising

Under simple interest, the interest multiple grows linearly. Going from 1× to 7× took 6 units of growth in 15 years, so each unit of growth takes 2.5 years. Reaching 10× needs 9 units of growth: \(9 \times 2.5 = 22.5\) years. Same answer, ten seconds.

Compound interest

Under compound interest, the interest earned in each period is added to the principal, and that larger balance earns interest in the next period. Interest earns interest. Each row multiplies the previous balance by the same factor \((1+i)\). Repeated multiplication is exactly what an exponent encodes, so after \(n\) periods:

$$\boxed{\;F = P(1+i)^n\;}$$

This single line is the seed of the entire subject. Every one of the seven formulas is either this equation rearranged, or this equation summed over a series.

Simple vs compound — the gap widens

Rs. 100 at 15% for 3 years. Simple: 15 + 15 + 15 = Rs. 45 interest → Rs. 145. Compound: 15.00, then 17.25, then 19.84 → Rs. 152.09. Only Rs. 7 apart over 3 years, but over 25 years at 20% the compound result is roughly ninety-five times the principal while simple interest gives six times. Time is the multiplier that matters.

Compound Interest · Q1

Find the amount if Rs. 20,000 is invested at 10% p.a. for 3 years.

$$F = P(1+i)^n = 20000\,(1.10)^3 = 20000 \times 1.331$$

Answer

\(F = \) Rs. 26,620

Compound Interest · Q2

The compound interest on a sum of Rs. 625 in 2 years is Rs. 51. Find the rate of interest.

Read carefully: Rs. 51 is the interest, not the amount. So \(F = 625 + 51 = 676\). Now we are solving for \(i\) with \(n\) known, which means taking a root rather than a logarithm:

$$676 = 625(1+i)^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; (1+i)^2 = 1.0816 \;\Longrightarrow\; 1+i = \sqrt{1.0816} = 1.04$$

Answer

\(i = \) 4% per annum

Compound Interest · Q3

At what rate of compound interest per annum does Rs. 1,200 become Rs. 1,348.32 in 2 years?

$$(1+i)^2 = \frac{1348.32}{1200} = 1.1236 \;\Longrightarrow\; 1+i = 1.06$$

Answer

\(i = \) 6% per annum

Compound Interest · Q4

You are considering investing $3,000 at 8% compounded annually for five years, or investing $3,000 at 9% per year simple interest for five years. Which is better?

Why this is a real question and not a trick. The simple-interest option offers a higher stated rate. Intuition says take the bigger number. But compounding is a structural advantage that can beat a rate advantage given enough periods. The only way to know is to compute both.

Option A — 8% compounded:

$$F = 3000(1.08)^5 = 3000 \times 1.46933 = \$4{,}407.98$$

Option B — 9% simple:

$$F = 3000(1 + 0.09 \times 5) = 3000 \times 1.45 = \$4{,}350.00$$

Answer

The 8% compound option wins, by $57.98. Compounding overcomes the 1% rate deficit by year 5.

The insight to carry forward

Where is the crossover? At \(n=4\): compound gives $4,081, simple gives $4,080 — essentially a tie. Before year 4, simple interest is ahead; after it, compounding pulls away permanently and the gap accelerates. A lower compound rate always beats a higher simple rate eventually. The exam version of this question is always set past the crossover.

Section 1Cash Flow Diagrams

What a cash flow diagram is, and why it exists

A problem in engineering economics arrives as a paragraph of English: someone deposits this, withdraws that, starting in some year, for some number of years, at some rate. The paragraph is where mistakes are made. A cash flow diagram (CFD) is a graphical translation of that paragraph — a picture of every rupee entering and leaving, placed on a timeline.

Your deck defines it simply: cash flow diagrams are the simple representation of income and outlay, and problems involving the time value of money can be conveniently represented in graphic form using them.

Why you should always draw one — even when you think you don't need to

The seven formulas each assume a very specific shape: a single amount here, a uniform series there, starting at a particular period. You cannot tell which formula applies until you can see the shape. More importantly, the most common exam error is not arithmetic — it is putting a series in the wrong place on the timeline, which silently corrupts every subsequent step. The diagram is the error-check. Draw it before you write a single factor.

How to draw one — the four rules

  1. Define the time frame and draw the horizontal axis. This is the timeline. Divide it into equal interest periods — usually years — and mark them 0, 1, 2, … , n. Time 0 is "now". The interest rate \(i\%\) is written above the line, because it governs the whole diagram.
  2. Draw arrows at the periods where money moves. Arrow length is roughly proportional to magnitude (it need not be to scale, but a Rs. 50,000 arrow should visibly dwarf a Rs. 2,500 one). Label each arrow with its amount.
  3. Point the arrows by sign. Upward arrows = positive flows = receipts (money coming in). Downward arrows = negative flows = disbursements (money going out).
  4. Net simultaneous flows into one arrow. If Rs. 5,000 comes in and Rs. 2,000 goes out at the end of year 4, you draw a single upward arrow of Rs. 3,000. The arrows represent net cash flows.

The anatomy of a cash flow diagram: timeline, interest rate, up-arrows for receipts, down-arrows for disbursements.

The end-of-period convention

In reality, cash moves at the beginning of a period, the middle, or any arbitrary instant. Modelling that faithfully would require handling partial-period interest, which would make hand calculation intolerable. So engineering economics adopts a simplifying assumption called the end-of-period convention: every cash flow is placed at the end of the interest period in which it occurs.

This is not merely a convenience — it is a definitional commitment that all seven formulas depend on. Note its most important consequence:

The end of period 1 IS the beginning of period 2

These are the same instant on the timeline. So a phrase like "withdrawals begin at the beginning of year 4" means the first withdrawal sits at the end of year 3, i.e. at \(t=3\). A phrase like "deposits at the end of each year for 5 years starting next year" means \(t = 1,2,3,4,5\). Mis-reading this by one period is the single most expensive mistake in this subject, because a one-period shift changes every factor exponent downstream.

Viewpoint: the same transaction, two diagrams

A cash flow diagram is drawn from someone's perspective. A loan is an inflow to the borrower on day one and an outflow from the lender on the same day. The two diagrams are exact mirror images — every arrow flips.

The same loan, two viewpoints. Perfect mirror images — this is why equivalence is preserved regardless of point of view.

Equivalence is maintained regardless of viewpoint

This is one of the four general principles of equivalence in your deck. The others: (i) comparing alternatives requires a common time basis — you must move every flow to one chosen instant, usually \(t=0\) (present worth) or \(t=n\) (future worth); (ii) equivalence depends on the interest rate — change \(i\) and the equivalence is destroyed, because equivalence is a function of magnitude, timing and rate; (iii) equivalence calculations may require converting a multiple-payment cash flow into a single cash flow.

Decision dilemma from your deck — why equivalence matters

A suburban Chicago couple won the Powerball. They had to choose between a single lump sum of $104 million, or $198 million paid out over 25 years ($7.92 million per year). They took the lump sum. Did they choose correctly?

The undiscounted totals say the instalments are worth nearly twice as much. But the instalments are spread over 25 years, and \(7.92\text{M}(P/A, i, 25)\) exceeds $104M only when \(i\) is below roughly 6.2%. Above that rate, the lump sum wins. The question has no answer until an interest rate is specified — which is precisely the second principle of equivalence: equivalence depends on the interest rate.

Three ways to repay the same loan

Your deck shows a bank loan of $20,000 for \(N = 5\) years at \(i = 9\%\), repaid three different ways. All three are economically equivalent at 9% — yet the total cash paid differs by over $5,000. Equivalence, not the total, is what the lender cares about.

The five types of cash flow

Every diagram you will ever draw is built from five primitive patterns. Recognising which one you are looking at is what selects your formula.

Categories of cash flows in engineering projects

When a project description is given in words, the expenses and receipts almost always fall into these five buckets. Recognising the bucket tells you the sign and the timing:

CategoryWhat it isSign & typical placement
First costExpense to build or to buy and installNegative, at \(t=0\) (single arrow down)

Worked CFD examples from your deck

CFD · Example 1

If $1,500 is invested now, $1,800 two years from now, and $2,000 four years from now at 6% compounded annually, what will be the total amount in 15 years? Draw the CFD.

Reading the description. Three separate investments — this is an irregular series, so each flow is drawn as its own arrow. All three are outflows from the investor (money leaving to be invested), and the unknown \(F\) at year 15 is the inflow.

The key observation for the arithmetic: the number of periods each arrow must travel is 15 minus its own position, not 15. The $1,500 compounds for 15 years, the $1,800 for 13, the $2,000 for 11.

$$F = 1500(F/P,6\%,15) + 1800(F/P,6\%,13) + 2000(F/P,6\%,11)$$

$$= 1500(2.3966) + 1800(2.1329) + 2000(1.8983) = 3594.9 + 3839.2 + 3796.6$$

Answer

\(F \approx\) $11,230.7

CFD · Example 2

A student buys an automobile costing $12,000, borrowing the money from a bank and paying it off in five equal annual instalments at 15% interest. Prepare a cash flow diagram.

Whose viewpoint? The student's. She receives the loan value at \(t=0\) (or equivalently receives the car), then pays out five equal amounts. This is a single present amount converting into a uniform series — the capital recovery shape, which we will meet as Formula 6.

$$A = P(A/P,15\%,5) = 12000 \times 0.29832 = \$3{,}579.8 \text{ per year}$$

CFD · Example 3 — two different series

An electrical engineer wants to deposit an amount \(P\) now such that she can withdraw \(A_1 = \$2{,}000\) per year for the first 5 years, and a different annual withdrawal of \(A_2 = \$3{,}000\) per year for the following 3 years. How would the cash flow diagram appear if \(i = 8.5\%\) per year?

The shape to notice: two separate uniform series back to back. Each will need its own \(P/A\) factor, and the second series — because it does not start at period 1 — will additionally need a \(P/F\) to shift it back. That two-step move is the heart of Section 3.

CFD · Example 4 — netting simultaneous flows

Arun buys a car, making an initial payment of Rs. 1,00,000 and taking a loan of Rs. 1,50,000 from ICFC Bank. He makes equal monthly repayments of Rs. 8,000 to clear the loan in full over 2 years. After the last payment he sells the car for Rs. 1,50,000. Draw two CFDs — one for Arun, one for ICFC Bank.

Arun's diagram. At \(t=0\) two things happen at once: he pays Rs. 1,00,000 down and receives Rs. 1,50,000 of loan. Applying rule 4, these net to a single upward arrow of Rs. 50,000 — though for clarity in a written answer it is acceptable to show both. Then 24 monthly outflows of Rs. 8,000, then a Rs. 1,50,000 inflow at month 24 from the sale.

ICFC Bank's diagram is the exact mirror for the loan portion only: Rs. 1,50,000 down-arrow at \(t=0\), and 24 up-arrows of Rs. 8,000. The down payment and the car sale are Arun's business, not the bank's — a viewpoint diagram only contains flows that cross that party's boundary. This is the detail most students miss.

CFD · Example 5 — a full project

A new plant to produce steel tubing requires an initial investment of $10 million now and $1 million for installing machinery during the first year. At the end of the 3rd year an additional $5 million is required, and after six years another $3 million. Annual operating costs are $3 million and annual revenues are $8 million. The life of the plant is 10 years. Draw the cash flow diagram.

Decomposition into categories. First cost: $10M at \(t=0\), plus $1M installation at \(t=1\). Overhauls / additional capital: $5M at \(t=3\), $3M at \(t=6\). O&M: $3M/year. Revenue: $8M/year. Since revenue and operating cost occur in the same periods, rule 4 nets them: a uniform series of \(8 - 3 = +\$5\)M per year — this is the number you actually put on the diagram.

Note year 1 carries two arrows: the +5M net operating flow and the −1M installation. They can be drawn separately (clearer) or netted to +4M.

CFD · Example 6 — the "past" diagram

A rental company spent $2,500 on a new air compressor 7 years ago. The annual rental income from the compressor has been $750. The $100 spent on maintenance the first year has increased each year by $25. The company plans to sell the compressor at the end of next year for $150. Construct the cash flow diagram from the company's perspective and indicate where the present worth now is located.

What makes this one different. "Now" is not at the left edge of the diagram. The timeline runs from \(-7\) (the purchase) to \(+1\) (the sale), and the present-worth location is at \(t = 0\), sitting in the middle. This is worth practising because exam problems love to place "now" somewhere other than the start.

The maintenance flows form a gradient series: $100, $125, $150, … with \(G = 25\). Rental income is a uniform series of $750. So the diagram carries three overlapping patterns simultaneously.

CFD · Example 9 — Bonka Toys robot

Bonka Toys is considering a robot that will cost $20,000 to buy. After 7 years its salvage value will be $2,000. An overhaul costing $5,000 will be needed in Year 4. O&M costs will be $2,500 per year. Draw the cash flow diagram.

CFD · Example 10 — boring machines (comparing two alternatives)

A company needs boring machine capacity. The choice is one fully automatic tape-controlled machine or two manually controlled machines.

Two things to be careful about

(i) Costs, not profits. Both alternatives produce the same output, so revenues are identical and irrelevant — this is the "differences among alternatives" principle from Section 0. Compare costs only, and pick the smaller present worth. Signs are all treated as positive costs here, with salvage subtracted.

(ii) The year-10 figure. Your faculty's diagram shows $21,500 at year 10. Section 2The Seven Interest Formulas

All seven follow one naming convention. The factor \((X/Y, i, n)\) means: "give me X, given Y". So \((F/P, i, n)\) finds \(F\) when you know \(P\). You multiply the known quantity by the factor and the letters cancel like fractions:

$$F = P \cdot \left(\frac{F}{P}, i, n\right) \qquad \text{— the } P \text{ cancels, leaving } F.$$

This cancellation trick is your safety net

If you ever write \(F = A(P/A, i, n)\), the letters give \(F = A \cdot P/A = P\) — you have produced a \(P\), not an \(F\). The units don't match, so the line is wrong. Check the letter algebra on every line you write. It catches the majority of formula-selection errors instantly.

Formula 1 · Single-Payment Compound Amount (F/P)

Objective

To find the single future sum \(F\) of an initial payment \(P\) after \(n\) periods at interest rate \(i\%\) compounded every period.

$$F = P(1+i)^n = P\,(F/P, i, n)$$

\((F/P, i, n)\) is called the single-payment compound amount factor.

One amount in at time 0, one amount out at time n. This factor pushes money forward in time.

Where it comes from. This is the compound interest recursion of Section 0, unchanged. Each period multiplies the balance by \((1+i)\); \(n\) periods multiply it by \((1+i)^n\). Because \((1+i) > 1\), this factor is always greater than 1 — moving money forward in time always increases its magnitude. That sanity check alone catches sign and direction errors.

F/P · Q1

A person deposits Rs. 50,000 at an interest rate of 18% compounded annually for 10 years. Find the maturity value after 10 years.

Identify the shape. One deposit, one withdrawal, ten years apart. Single cash flow → \(F/P\).

P = 50,000i = 18%n = 10F = ?

$$F = P(F/P, 18\%, 10) = 50000 \times (1.18)^{10} = 50000 \times 5.2338$$

Answer

\(F = \) Rs. 2,61,690 (computed precisely: Rs. 2,61,691.78)

Note the scale: at 18%, money grows more than fivefold in a decade. That is the practical meaning of "time value".

F/P · Q2

How much money will be accumulated in 25 years if Rs. 800 is deposited one year from now, Rs. 2,400 six years from now, and Rs. 3,300 eight years from now, all at an interest rate of 18% p.a.?

The critical step is counting periods correctly. The target is year 25. Each deposit compounds for 25 minus its own year, not 25:

  • Rs. 800 sits at year 1 → travels \(25 - 1 = 24\) years
  • Rs. 2,400 sits at year 6 → travels \(25 - 6 = 19\) years
  • Rs. 3,300 sits at year 8 → travels \(25 - 8 = 17\) years

$$F = 800(F/P,18\%,24) + 2400(F/P,18\%,19) + 3300(F/P,18\%,17)$$

$$= 800(62.669) + 2400(23.214) + 3300(17.673)$$

$$= 50{,}135 + 55{,}714 + 58{,}321$$

Answer

\(F \approx\) Rs. 1,53,220

The mistake to avoid

Students routinely write \((F/P, 18\%, 25)\) for the Rs. 800 because "25 years" is in the question. But the deposit is not made now — it is made at year 1, so it has only 24 years to grow. Always compute \(n = \text{target year} - \text{flow's year}\).

F/P · Q5 — solving for n (GE stock)

Formula 2 · Single-Payment Present Worth (P/F)

Objective

To find the present worth \(P\) of a single future sum \(F\) received after \(n\) periods at interest rate \(i\%\) compounded at the end of every interest period.

$$P = \frac{F}{(1+i)^n} = F\,(P/F, i, n)$$

\((P/F, i, n)\) is called the single-payment present worth factor.

This is Formula 1 solved for \(P\). Nothing new is being claimed — it is algebra. But the interpretation is genuinely different and worth dwelling on. Formula 1 asks "what will this grow into?"; Formula 2 asks "what is a future promise worth today?" That second question is called discounting, and it is how every investment decision is actually made.

Why \((P/F)\) is always less than 1

Since \((1+i)^n > 1\), its reciprocal is less than 1. A future rupee is always worth less than a rupee today. The further away and the higher the rate, the smaller the factor. At 15% over 10 years the factor is 0.2472 — a promised rupee a decade out is worth about 25 paise now. If your \(P\) comes out larger than your \(F\), you have used the wrong factor.

Formula 3 · Equal-Payment Series Compound Amount (F/A)

Objective

To find the future worth of \(n\) equal payments \(A\) made at the end of every interest period until the end of the \(n\)-th period, at interest rate \(i\%\) compounded each period.

$$F = A\left[\frac{(1+i)^n - 1}{i}\right] = A\,(F/A, i, n)$$

\((F/A, i, n)\) is called the equal-payment series compound amount factor, or uniform series compound amount factor.

Note carefully: F lands at the SAME instant as the last deposit A. The final A earns no interest.

Derivation — worth understanding, not memorising

Each deposit compounds for a different length of time, so let us count. The deposit at the end of period 1 has \(n-1\) periods left to grow; the one at period 2 has \(n-2\); the last one at period \(n\) has zero periods and is worth exactly \(A\). Summing:

$$F = A(1+i)^{n-1} + A(1+i)^{n-2} + \cdots + A(1+i)^1 + A \qquad (1)$$

This is a geometric series, and the standard trick is to multiply through by the common ratio:

$$F(1+i) = A(1+i)^{n} + A(1+i)^{n-1} + \cdots + A(1+i)^2 + A(1+i) \qquad (2)$$

Subtracting (1) from (2), every middle term cancels — the whole point of the manoeuvre:

$$F(1+i) - F = A(1+i)^n - A \;\Longrightarrow\; Fi = A\left[(1+i)^n - 1\right]$$

$$\boxed{\;F = A\left[\frac{(1+i)^n-1}{i}\right]\;}$$

The single most important structural fact about F/A

The last payment coincides with \(F\) and earns nothing. There are \(n\) payments and the future worth is taken at period \(n\), not period \(n+1\). Equivalently: if the series runs from year \(k\) to year \(m\), then \(n = m - k + 1\) and the raw \(F/A\) result lands at year \(m\). Almost every mistake in Section 3 traces back to forgetting where the raw \(F/A\) answer sits.

F/A · Q6 — the big savings plan

You have a savings plan covering the next ten years: you put aside $600 today, $800 at the end of every year for the next five years, and $2,000 at the end of each year for the remaining five years. You expect to withdraw $300 at the end of every year for the first 3 years, and $350 at the end of every year thereafter till the 10th year. In addition you withdraw $50 at the end of every 2 years till the 15th year. Interest is 12%. Find the net position at year 15.

This is the hardest bookkeeping problem in your material. Nothing in it is conceptually new — but it stacks five separate series with different start points, different end points, and different periods. Work it in strict order and it becomes routine.

The $50 biennial withdrawals fall at odd years 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15 (first at year 1, every 2 years thereafter).

Outflows (money deposited), valued at year 15

(a) $600 at year 0 — a single flow travelling 15 years:

$$600(F/P,12\%,15) = 600 \times 5.4736 = 3{,}284.2$$

(b) $800 at years 1–5 — a 5-payment series landing at year 5, then pushed forward 10 years:

$$800(F/A,12\%,5)(F/P,12\%,10) = 800(6.3528)(3.1058) = 15{,}783.4$$

(c) $2,000 at years 6–10 — a 5-payment series landing at year 10, then pushed forward 5 years:

$$2000(F/A,12\%,5)(F/P,12\%,5) = 2000(6.3528)(1.7623) = 22{,}393.1$$

$$\textbf{Total outflow at year 15} = 3{,}284.2 + 15{,}783.4 + 22{,}393.1 = 41{,}460.7$$

Inflows (money withdrawn), valued at year 15

(d) $300 at years 1–3 — lands at year 3, pushed forward 12 years:

$$300(F/A,12\%,3)(F/P,12\%,12) = 300(3.3744)(3.8960) = 3{,}944.0$$

(e) $350 at years 4–10 — that is 7 payments (\(10-4+1=7\)), landing at year 10, pushed forward 5 years:

$$350(F/A,12\%,7)(F/P,12\%,5) = 350(10.089)(1.7623) = 6{,}223.1$$

(f) $50 at years 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15 — not a uniform annual series (the gap is 2 years), so each is handled individually:

$$50\big[(F/P,12\%,14)+(F/P,12\%,12)+(F/P,12\%,10)+(F/P,12\%,8)+(F/P,12\%,6)+(F/P,12\%,4)+(F/P,12\%,2)+1\big]$$

$$= 50[4.8871+3.8960+3.1058+2.4760+1.9738+1.5735+1.2544+1] = 50 \times 20.167 = 1{,}008.3$$

$$\textbf{Total inflow at year 15} = 3{,}944.0 + 6{,}223.1 + 1{,}008.3 = 11{,}175.4$$

Answer

Net cash flow at year 15 \(= 11{,}175.4 - 41{,}460.7 = \) −$30,285.3  (the source slide states −$30,437.98 — its individual line-item values match what's derived above, but its own final addition/subtraction doesn't; the figure here follows from the displayed numbers)

The negative sign means the plan is net accumulating: you have put in $30,438 more (in year-15 terms) than you have taken out, so that is your closing balance.

How to survive a problem like this in an exam

Do not attempt it in one line. Make a table with four columns — flow, years it occupies, factor chain, value at target — and fill it in row by row. You will lose marks for a wrong factor; you will lose the whole question for losing track of which flows you have already handled.

The one genuinely non-obvious step is (f): a "every 2 years" pattern is not a uniform series at \(i = 12\%\) annual. Either list the flows individually (as above), or convert to an effective 2-year rate \(i_2 = (1.12)^2 - 1 = 25.44\%\) and use \(F/A\) with \(n=8\) at 25.44%. Both give the same answer; the first is safer under time pressure.

F/A · Q7 — end-of-period vs beginning-of-period (annuity due)

Suppose you make an annual contribution of $3,000 to your savings account at the end of each year for 10 years. If the account earns 7% annually, how much can be withdrawn at the end of 10 years? Second part: suppose the ten deposits were made at the beginning of each period instead — what is the balance at the end of period 10?

Part (a) — the ordinary annuity. Deposits at the end of years 1–10, balance wanted at year 10. Textbook alignment:

$$F = 3000(F/A,7\%,10) = 3000 \times 13.8164 = \$41{,}449.34$$

Part (b) — the annuity due. Deposits now sit at the beginning of years 1–10, i.e. at times \(t = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, 9\). Every deposit has been shifted one period earlier, so every deposit earns exactly one extra year of interest. There is no need for a new formula — just multiply the ordinary result by \((1+i)\):

$$F = 3000(F/A,7\%,10)(1.07) = 41449.34 \times 1.07$$

Answer

(a) $41,449.34  ·  (b) $44,350.80

The general rule for beginning-of-period series

$$F_{\text{annuity due}} = A\,(F/A,i,n)\,(1+i) \qquad\qquad P_{\text{annuity due}} = A\,(P/A,i,n)\,(1+i)$$

Depositing at the start of each year rather than the end gains you $2,901 here — a 7% improvement, which is exactly one year's interest. This is not a coincidence: it is the whole difference, and it is why the end-of-period convention must be stated explicitly whenever a question hinges on it.

You will meet the same idea in Section 3, where retirement withdrawals are taken "at the beginning of each year" — the identical adjustment applies there.

Formula 4 · Equal-Payment Series Sinking Fund (A/F)

Objective

To find the equal amount \(A\) that must be deposited at the end of every interest period for \(n\) periods in order to realise a future sum \(F\) at the end of the \(n\)-th period at interest rate \(i\%\).

$$A = F\left[\frac{i}{(1+i)^n - 1}\right] = F\,(A/F, i, n)$$

\((A/F, i, n)\) is called the equal-payment series sinking fund factor.

This is Formula 3 inverted — literally the reciprocal: \((A/F, i, n) = 1/(F/A, i, n)\). But the question it answers is completely different and much more practically common. \(F/A\) asks "I'm saving this much — what will I end up with?" \(A/F\) asks "I need this much by then — how much must I save each year?" The second is the question real people and real companies actually face.

Why it's called a "sinking fund"

The term comes from corporate finance. When a company issues bonds that mature in 20 years, it cannot simply hope to have the money in year 20 — so it sets aside a fixed amount annually into a dedicated fund that "sinks" the debt. Machinery replacement reserves work identically: you know the machine dies in 15 years, you know the replacement cost, so you reverse-engineer the annual set-aside. Whenever a problem says "replace", "reserve", "accumulate by", or "set aside", reach for \(A/F\).

Formula 5 · Equal-Payment Series Present Worth (P/A)

Objective

To find the present worth of an equal payment \(A\) made at the end of every interest period for \(n\) periods.

$$P = A\left[\frac{(1+i)^n - 1}{i(1+i)^n}\right] = A\,(P/A, i, n)$$

\((P/A, i, n)\) is called the equal-payment series present worth factor.

Critical positioning: P sits ONE PERIOD BEFORE the first A. If the series starts at year k, the raw P/A result lands at year k−1.

Where it comes from. You do not need a fresh derivation — chain two factors you already have. Take the series to its future worth, then discount that back to time 0:

$$P = F(P/F,i,n) = A\left[\frac{(1+i)^n-1}{i}\right] \cdot \frac{1}{(1+i)^n} = A\left[\frac{(1+i)^n-1}{i(1+i)^n}\right]$$

The P/A positioning rule — the single most examined subtlety in this subject

\((P/A, i, n)\) places the present worth one period before the first payment of the series, not at the year you happen to be thinking about. If a series of withdrawals runs from year 3 to year 7, then \(A(P/A,i,5)\) gives you a value sitting at year 2 — and you must then apply \((P/F, i, 2)\) to bring it to year 0.

Say this to yourself every time: "P/A lands one step behind the first arrow."

Formula 6 · Equal-Payment Series Capital Recovery (A/P)

Objective

To find the annual equivalent amount \(A\) which is to be recovered at the end of every interest period for \(n\) periods, for a loan \(P\) sanctioned now at interest rate \(i\%\) compounded every period.

$$A = P\left[\frac{i(1+i)^n}{(1+i)^n - 1}\right] = P\,(A/P, i, n)$$

\((A/P, i, n)\) is called the equal-payment series capital recovery factor.

This is Formula 5 inverted: \((A/P, i, n) = 1/(P/A, i, n)\). It is the single most commercially important formula in the subject — it is what computes your EMI. Every home loan, car loan and equipment loan in the world is priced with this factor.

Why "capital recovery"

From the lender's side, \(A\) is the annual amount needed to recover the capital \(P\) plus the required return \(i\) over \(n\) years. From the borrower's side it is the instalment. Note that \((A/P, i, n) > i\) always — the payment must cover the interest and chip away at the principal. As \(n \to \infty\), \((A/P) \to i\): with an infinite loan you would pay interest forever and never repay principal. That limiting behaviour is a good intuition check.

$$\frac{1-(1.1)^{-n}}{0.10} = 3.75 \;\Longrightarrow\; 1 - (1.1)^{-n} = 0.375 \;\Longrightarrow\; (1.1)^{-n} = 0.625$$

Formula 7 · Uniform Gradient Series Annual Equivalent (A/G)

Objective

To find the annual equivalent amount of a series with an amount \(A_1\) at the end of the first year and an equal increment \(G\) at the end of each of the following \(n-1\) years, with interest rate \(i\%\) compounded annually.

$$A = A_1 + G\left[\frac{(1+i)^n - in - 1}{i(1+i)^n - i}\right] = A_1 + G\,(A/G, i, n)$$

\((A/G, i, n)\) is called the uniform gradient series factor.

The three facts that make gradient problems tractable
  1. The gradient starts in year 2, not year 1. Year 1 carries the base amount \(A_1\) alone. This is why the factor uses \(n\) (total periods) but the increments number only \(n-1\).
  2. \(A/G\) converts the gradient into a level annuity — it does not give you \(P\) or \(F\) directly. The output \(A\) is an equivalent uniform series. You must then apply \(P/A\) or \(F/A\) to get where you actually want to go. A/G is always step one of at least two.
  3. For a decreasing series, subtract: \(A = A_1 - G(A/G, i, n)\). Same factor, opposite sign.
A/G · Q1 — John and Barbara

John and Barbara have opened two savings accounts earning 10% annual interest. John will deposit $1,000 at the end of the first year and increase this by $300 for each of the next five years. Barbara wants to make an equal annual deposit so that the two accounts have equal balances at the end of six years. What should Barbara's annual deposit be?

Reading the structure. John's deposits are $1,000, $1,300, $1,600, $1,900, $2,200, $2,500 over years 1–6. So \(A_1 = 1000\), \(G = 300\), \(n = 6\).

What Barbara's deposit must be. Two series have equal balances at year 6 if and only if they are equivalent — and the uniform series equivalent to John's gradient is Barbara's answer. No \(F/A\) is needed at all; \(A/G\) delivers it directly.

$$A = A_1 + G(A/G,10\%,6) = 1000 + 300(2.2236)$$

$$= 1000 + 667.08$$

Answer

Barbara's annual deposit = $1,667.08

Sanity check

John's deposits average \((1000+2500)/2 = \$1{,}750\). Barbara's equivalent is $1,667 — slightly less than the arithmetic average, which is exactly right: John's larger deposits come later and therefore earn less interest, so a level series can be smaller and still match. If your \(A/G\) answer ever exceeds the arithmetic mean of an increasing series, you have made an error.

$$-20(P/F,10\%,1) = -20(0.9091) = -18.18$$

Term 2 — the $20 outflows at years 6–12. That is \(12 - 6 + 1 = 7\) payments; \((P/A)\) lands at year 5, so discount 5 more periods:

$$-20(P/A,10\%,7)(P/F,10\%,5) = -20(4.8684)(0.6209) = -60.46$$

Term 3 — the gradient inflows at years 3, 4, 5. \(A_1 = 20\), \(G = 20\), \(n = 3\). Level it, take \(P/A\) (which lands at year 2), then discount 2 periods:

$$\big[20 + 20(A/G,10\%,3)\big](P/A,10\%,3)(P/F,10\%,2)$$

$$= [20 + 20(0.9366)](2.4869)(0.8264) = 38.73 \times 2.4869 \times 0.8264 = +79.63$$

Sum: \(-18.18 - 60.46 + 79.63\)

Answer

\(PW \approx\) $0.96  (slide value: 0.9614)

What an answer this close to zero means

The present worth is essentially nil, meaning the inflows and outflows are almost exactly economically equivalent at 10%. In project-evaluation language, 10% is very nearly the internal rate of return of this cash flow. A near-zero answer here is a sign you did it right, not a sign something cancelled by accident.

Section 3Problems Using Multiple Formulas

Every problem in this section is built from the seven factors you already know. What makes them harder is that the cash flows do not sit where the formulas expect them. There is a single universal method:

The universal method for any multi-formula problem
  1. Draw the cash flow diagram. Non-negotiable. Mark every year number.
  2. Choose one focal date — the year at which you will equate everything. Pick the one that minimises work: usually the year where the unknown lives, or where most flows already sit.
  3. Identify each pattern separately (single flow / uniform series / gradient) and note the years it occupies.
  4. For each pattern, write a factor chain that moves it to the focal date. Series factors first (they collapse the pattern to a point), then a single-payment factor to slide that point to the focal date.
  5. Write the equivalence equation — inflows = outflows, or known = unknown — and solve.

The rule that governs step 4: \(F/A\) lands at the last payment. \(P/A\) lands one period before the first payment. \(A/G\) produces a series occupying the same years as the gradient. Get these three right and the rest is arithmetic.

3.1 · Deferred and shifted series

Multi · Q1 — Biogen's deferred loan

Biogen Co. has borrowed $250,000 to purchase lab equipment at 8% interest, repaid in equal instalments over the next 6 years. (a) Compute the annual instalment. (b) Suppose Biogen negotiates to defer the first loan payment until the end of year 2 (still paying 6 instalments at 8%). What should the annual instalment be?

(a) The standard case. Payments at years 1–6, loan at year 0 — textbook \(A/P\) alignment:

$$A = 250000(A/P,8\%,6) = 250000 \times 0.21632 = \$54{,}080$$

(b) The deferred case — and the reasoning that makes it easy. Payments now occur at years 2–7. There are two equally valid routes; understanding both is worth the effort.

Route 1 — move the loan forward to meet the series

The payments run years 2–7, so their \(P/A\) value lands at year 1. Rather than dragging the series backward, push the loan forward one year to year 1, where it has grown by one year of interest:

$$P_{(1)} = 250000(F/P,8\%,1) = 250000 \times 1.08 = \$270{,}000$$

Now the loan and the series both sit at year 1, so apply \(A/P\) directly:

$$A = 270000(A/P,8\%,6) = 270000 \times 0.21632 = \$58{,}405$$

Route 2 — discount the series back to year 0

$$250000 = A(P/A,8\%,6)(P/F,8\%,1) = A(4.6229)(0.9259) = 4.2805A$$

$$A = 250000/4.2805 = \$58{,}405 \quad \checkmark \text{ same answer}$$

Answer

(a) $54,080 per year  ·  (b) $58,405 per year

The economic reading

Deferring the first payment by one year raises each of the six instalments by $4,325 — a total of about $26,000 extra. Deferral is never free. The bank charges you a full year of interest on the whole principal for the privilege of waiting.

Multi · Q2 — the college fund

A couple with a newborn daughter wants to save for college. The fund pays 7% annual interest. Assuming the child enters college at age 18, the parents estimate $40,000 per year will be required for 4 years. Determine the equal annual amounts the couple must save. The first deposit is on the child's first birthday, the last on her 18th birthday; the first withdrawal is on her 18th birthday.

Map the timeline precisely — this is where the problem is won or lost.

  • Deposits: birthdays 1 through 18 → 18 deposits, last one at \(t = 18\).
  • Withdrawals: $40,000 at birthdays 18, 19, 20, 21 → 4 withdrawals, first one at \(t=18\).

The critical observation: the last deposit and the first withdrawal happen on the same day (the 18th birthday). So year 18 is the natural focal date.

Step 1 — value the withdrawals at year 18. The withdrawal at \(t=18\) needs no discounting. The remaining three (years 19, 20, 21) form a 3-payment series whose \(P/A\) lands at year 18:

$$P_{18} = 40000 + 40000(P/A,7\%,3) = 40000 + 40000(2.6243) = 40000 + 104{,}972 = \$144{,}972$$

Step 2 — find the deposit that accumulates to $144,972 by year 18. The deposits form an 18-payment series ending exactly at year 18, so \(A/F\) applies with no shift:

$$A = 144972(A/F,7\%,18) = 144972 \times 0.029405$$

Answer

\(A = \) $4,262.17 per year

The trap

If you carelessly write \(40000(P/A,7\%,4)\) you get the value at year 17, not year 18 — because \(P/A\) lands one period before the first payment. You would then need an extra \((F/P,7\%,1)\). The cleaner habit is to split off the payment that coincides with your focal date and treat the rest as a series, exactly as done above.

3.2 · Equating two series — solving for an unknown amount

Multi · Q3 — find C (deposits vs withdrawals)

From the cash flow diagram, find the value of \(C\) that establishes economic equivalence between the deposit series and the withdrawal series at 8% compounded annually. Deposits of \(C\) occur at years 1, 2, 3 and again at years 6, 7, 8. Withdrawals of $6,000 occur at years 9 and 10.

Choose the focal date: year 10. Everything moves forward, which means only \(F/A\) and \(F/P\) — the two factors least prone to positioning errors.

Right side (withdrawals): two payments at years 9 and 10, so \((F/A,8\%,2)\) lands at year 10 with no shift:

$$\text{RHS} = 6000(F/A,8\%,2) = 6000 \times 2.08 = \$12{,}480$$

Left side (deposits): two separate 3-payment blocks.

  • Block 1 (years 1–3): \((F/A,8\%,3)\) lands at year 3 → push forward \(10-3=7\) years.
  • Block 2 (years 6–8): \((F/A,8\%,3)\) lands at year 8 → push forward \(10-8=2\) years.

$$\text{LHS} = C(F/A,8\%,3)(F/P,8\%,7) + C(F/A,8\%,3)(F/P,8\%,2)$$

$$= C(3.2464)(1.7138) + C(3.2464)(1.1664) = 5.5638C + 3.7866C = 9.3504C$$

Equate:

$$9.3504\,C = 12480 \;\Longrightarrow\; C = \frac{12480}{9.3504}$$

Answer

\(C = \) $1,334.72

Multi · Q4 — find C (inflows vs outflows)

What value of \(C\) makes the inflow series equivalent to the outflow series at 10%? Inflows: $300 at years 1–5 and $500 at years 6–8. Outflows: \(2C\) at year 0 and \(C\) at years 2 through 8.

Focal date: year 8.

Inflows:

  • $300 at years 1–5: \((F/A,10\%,5)\) lands at year 5 → push forward 3 years.
  • $500 at years 6–8: \((F/A,10\%,3)\) lands at year 8 → no shift.

$$\text{Inflow}_8 = 300(F/A,10\%,5)(F/P,10\%,3) + 500(F/A,10\%,3)$$

$$= 300(6.1051)(1.331) + 500(3.31) = 2{,}437.8 + 1{,}655.0 = 4{,}092.8$$

Outflows:

  • \(2C\) at year 0: a single flow travelling 8 years → \((F/P,10\%,8)\).
  • \(C\) at years 2–8: that is \(8-2+1 = 7\) payments, and \((F/A,10\%,7)\) lands at year 8 → no shift.

$$\text{Outflow}_8 = 2C(F/P,10\%,8) + C(F/A,10\%,7) = 2C(2.1436) + C(9.4872) = 13.7744C$$

$$13.7744\,C = 4092.8 \;\Longrightarrow\; C = \frac{4092.8}{13.7744}$$

Answer

\(C = \) $297.13

Why the year-0 flow uses F/P and not F/A

\(2C\) is a single payment, so it needs a single-payment factor. The \(C\) series is a uniform series, so it needs \(F/A\). Mixing these up — trying to fold the year-0 flow into the series — is the commonest error on this problem type. A payment at year 0 can never be part of an end-of-year series that starts at year 2.

Multi · Q5 — the X and 2X investment plan

A person plans to withdraw Rs. 50,000 from the 10th to the 15th year, and then for the next five years the annual withdrawal increases to Rs. 60,000. To fund this he invests \(X\) from the 2nd to the 5th year and \(2X\) from the 6th to the 8th year. Determine \(X\). Assume 12% interest.

First, count everything carefully.

  • Withdrawals: Rs. 50,000 at years 10–15 → \(15-10+1 = 6\) payments. Rs. 60,000 at years 16–20 → 5 payments.
  • Investments: \(X\) at years 2–5 → 4 payments. \(2X\) at years 6–8 → 3 payments.

Focal date: year 20.

Withdrawals valued at year 20

The Rs. 50,000 block ends at year 15, so \((F/A,12\%,6)\) lands there → push forward 5 years. The Rs. 60,000 block ends at year 20, so no shift.

$$F_{WD} = 50000(F/A,12\%,6)(F/P,12\%,5) + 60000(F/A,12\%,5)$$

$$= 50000(8.1152)(1.7623) + 60000(6.3528) = 715{,}092 + 381{,}168 = \text{Rs. } 10{,}96{,}260$$

Investments valued at year 20

Here is an elegant shortcut used in the model solution: instead of moving each block separately, collapse both blocks to a common intermediate point (year 5), then move that single value forward once.

  • \(X\) at years 2–5: \((F/A,12\%,4)\) lands exactly at year 5.
  • \(2X\) at years 6–8: starts at year 6, so \((P/A,12\%,3)\) lands at year 5.

Both are now at year 5. Push the combined value forward 15 years:

$$F_{IN} = \Big[X(F/A,12\%,4) + 2X(P/A,12\%,3)\Big](F/P,12\%,15)$$

$$= \big[X(4.7793) + 2X(2.4018)\big](5.4736) = \big[4.7793X + 4.8036X\big](5.4736) = 52.453\,X$$

Equate

$$52.453\,X = 10{,}96{,}260 \;\Longrightarrow\; X = \frac{1096260}{52.453}$$

Answer

\(X = \) Rs. 20,900 per year (years 2–5), and \(2X = \) Rs. 41,800 per year (years 6–8).
Slide values: X = 20,894.23 and 2X = 41,788.47.

The technique worth stealing

Notice how a forward-facing series (\(F/A\) at year 5) and a backward-facing series (\(P/A\) at year 5) were made to meet at the same instant. Whenever two series sit on either side of a convenient year, aim both at that year — it saves an entire factor and a lot of arithmetic.

3.3 · Retirement and education planning

Multi · Q6 — the boy's college fund (two interest rates)

A boy is now 11. On his fifth birthday he received $5,000 from his grandparents, invested in a 10-year fixed deposit at 6% compounded annually. His parents plan to have $6,000 available each year for his 19th to 22nd birthdays. The fixed deposit will be reinvested when it matures. How much equal amount should the parents deposit each year, starting now on his 11th birthday, so that one year after the last deposit they can start making payments? All future investments earn 6.5% compounded annually.

This problem is deliberately loaded with timeline traps. Work through them one at a time.

  • The $5,000 gift: deposited at age 5, matures at age 15 (10-year FD at 6%). After maturity it is reinvested at 6.5% until needed.
  • Parents' deposits: deposits begin now, at age 11. "One year after the last deposit they start making payments", and the first payment is at 19, so the last deposit is at age 18. Deposits at ages 11–18 → \(18-11+1 = 8\) deposits.
  • Withdrawals: $6,000 at ages 19, 20, 21, 22 → 4 payments.

Focal date: age 18 (the last deposit, one year before the first withdrawal).

Requirement at age 18

The withdrawals start at age 19, so \((P/A,6.5\%,4)\) lands at age 18 — exactly where we want it:

$$\text{Need}_{18} = 6000(P/A,6.5\%,4) = 6000 \times 3.4258 = \$20{,}555$$

Available at age 18

From the gift: $5,000 grows at 6% for 10 years (age 5 → 15), then at 6.5% for 3 more years (age 15 → 18):

$$F_{\text{gift}} = 5000(F/P,6\%,10)(F/P,6.5\%,3) = 5000(1.7908)(1.2079) = \$10{,}816$$

From the parents: 8 deposits ending at age 18, so \((F/A,6.5\%,8)\) lands there:

$$F_{\text{par}} = A(F/A,6.5\%,8) = A(10.0768)$$

Equate

$$6000(P/A,6.5\%,4) = 5000(F/P,6\%,10)(F/P,6.5\%,3) + A(F/A,6.5\%,8)$$

$$20{,}555 = 10{,}816 + 10.0768A \;\Longrightarrow\; A = \frac{9{,}739}{10.0768}$$

Answer

\(A = \) $966.42 per year

Two things this problem is testing

(i) Two different interest rates in one problem. The gift earns 6% for its FD term and 6.5% afterwards. You must switch rates at the right instant — chain \((F/P,6\%,10)\) then \((F/P,6.5\%,3)\), never a single blended rate.

(ii) "One year after the last deposit." This phrase exists purely to fix the deposit end date relative to the withdrawal start. Whenever you see it, work backwards from the first withdrawal.

Multi · Q7 — the 33/27 retirement plan

You plan to retire 33 years from now and expect to live 27 years after retiring. You want enough at retirement to withdraw Rs. 1,80,000 at the beginning of each year you expect to live, plus a lump sum of Rs. 25,00,000 at the time of your expected death (60 years from now). You will accumulate the fund by equal annual deposits at the end of each year for the next 33 years, earning 12% on your deposits but only 6% on your withdrawals. What equal annual deposits must you make?

The structure: two phases, two rates, one bridge. The bridge is year 33 — the retirement date. Everything before it grows at 12%; everything after it is discounted at 6%.

  • Withdrawals: Rs. 1,80,000 at the beginning of each retirement year. The first is at the start of retirement = year 33 itself. Then years 34, 35, …, 59 → that is 26 more.
  • Lump sum: Rs. 25,00,000 at year 60, which is \(60-33 = 27\) years after the bridge.

Step 1 — what must the fund be worth at year 33? (discount at 6%)

Split off the year-33 withdrawal (it needs no discounting), then treat the remaining 26 as a series whose \(P/A\) lands at year 33:

$$P_{33} = 180000 + 180000(P/A,6\%,26) + 2500000(P/F,6\%,27)$$

$$= 180000 + 180000(13.0032) + 2500000(0.2074)$$

$$= 180{,}000 + 23{,}40{,}576 + 5{,}18{,}500 \approx \text{Rs. } 30{,}39{,}000$$

Step 2 — what annual deposit accumulates that by year 33? (grow at 12%)

33 deposits ending at year 33 → \(A/F\) with no shift:

$$A = P_{33}(A/F,12\%,33) = 3039000 \times 0.00292$$

Answer

\(A \approx\) Rs. 8,875 per year  (slide value: Rs. 8,813.32 — the difference is factor-table rounding on a very small \(A/F\) factor)

Why the answer is so startlingly small

Rs. 8,875 per year for 33 years — a total of Rs. 2.93 lakh — funds a Rs. 30 lakh retirement corpus. Because \((A/F,12\%,33) = 0.00292\), 99.7% of the corpus is interest. This single number is the most persuasive argument for starting retirement saving early that this subject offers, and it is exactly the kind of remark that earns interpretation marks.

Note also the deliberate asymmetry: you earn 12% while accumulating but only 6% while withdrawing. That is realistic — retirees shift into safer, lower-yielding assets. It also means you cannot use one rate throughout.

Multi · Q8 — Mr Jones' retirement

Mr. Jones, 35, is planning for a 20-year retirement. He intends to withdraw $6,000 for his first five years of retirement, from ages 61 to 65. Then for another 15 years he intends to withdraw $10,000 yearly. For this purpose he plans to invest $10,000 at age 40. In addition, what equal payments does Jones need to invest for the next 20 years (starting at age 36) to cover his retirement plan? The bank gives 9%.

The clever move in this solution is how the withdrawals are decomposed. Rather than treating $6,000 (ages 61–65) and $10,000 (ages 66–80) as two separate blocks with different start points, notice:

Decomposition trick

$6,000 for ages 61–80 (20 years) plus an extra $4,000 for ages 66–80 (15 years) reproduces exactly the required pattern: $6,000 in the early years and $6,000 + $4,000 = $10,000 in the later years. Both blocks now end at age 80, so both use a bare \(F/A\) with no shifting at all.

Focal date: age 80.

Required at age 80

$$F_{\text{req}} = 6000(F/A,9\%,20) + 4000(F/A,9\%,15)$$

$$= 6000(51.160) + 4000(29.361) = 3{,}06{,}960 + 1{,}17{,}444 = \$4{,}24{,}404$$

Available at age 80

  • The $10,000 at age 40: a single flow growing for \(80-40 = 40\) years.
  • The unknown series \(A\): ages 36–55 (20 payments), so \((F/A,9\%,20)\) lands at age 55 → push forward \(80-55 = 25\) years.

$$F_{\text{avail}} = 10000(1.09)^{40} + A(F/A,9\%,20)(1.09)^{25}$$

$$= 10000(31.409) + A(51.160)(8.623) = 3{,}14{,}090 + 441.15A$$

$$4{,}24{,}404 = 3{,}14{,}090 + 441.15A \;\Longrightarrow\; 441.15A = 1{,}10{,}314$$

\(A = \) $250.05 per year

Note on the problem statement

The text says "$10,000 at the end of 40 year", which is ambiguous. Your faculty's diagram places it at age 40 (i.e. 5 years from now), and the factor \((1+i)^{40}\) confirms that reading, since \(80 - 40 = 40\). Follow the diagram.

3.4 · Loan restructuring

Multi · Q9 — early payoff of a loan

A company 12 years ago borrowed Rs. 4,00,000 for a new machine tool, agreeing to repay in 25 yearly equal payments at 12%. The company now wants to pay off the remaining loan amount completely, to be paid during the next instalment period. How much would this payment be, assuming no penalty for early payout?

The concept: outstanding balance = present worth of the remaining payments. This is a genuinely important idea — the balance you owe on any loan at any instant is simply the discounted value of everything you have not yet paid.

Step 1 — find the original instalment

$$A = 400000(A/P,12\%,25) = 400000 \times 0.1275 = \text{Rs. } 51{,}000$$

Step 2 — how many payments remain?

12 years have passed, so 12 payments have been made. Remaining: \(25 - 12 = 13\) payments, at years 13 through 25.

Step 3 — value them at the payoff date

The payoff happens "during the next instalment period", i.e. at year 13. Applying \((P/A,12\%,13)\) to the 13 remaining payments lands the value at year 12, so one more year of interest must be added:

$$P_{\text{bal}(13)} = 51000(P/A,12\%,13)(1+i) = 51000(6.4235)(1.12)$$

$$= 51000 \times 6.4235 \times 1.12$$

Answer

Payoff amount = Rs. 3,66,913  (slide value: Rs. 3,66,939)

The uncomfortable truth this reveals

After 12 years of a 25-year loan — nearly half the term — the company has repaid only about Rs. 33,000 of a Rs. 4,00,000 principal. Almost every rupee paid so far went to interest. This is the standard amortisation profile of a long high-rate loan, and it is why early payments barely dent the principal.

Multi · Q10 — the same loan, deferred start

In the above question, if the 25 equal payments were started after skipping the first two years, what would the equal payment be, with money growing at 12% per year?

The reasoning. The bank does not forgive two years of interest — the principal simply grows untouched for two years, and the 25-payment schedule is then applied to the larger amount. Payments run years 3 through 27.

Step 1 — grow the principal through the two idle years:

$$P_{(2)} = 400000(F/P,12\%,2) = 400000 \times 1.2544 = \text{Rs. } 5{,}01{,}760$$

Step 2 — the payments run years 3–27, so their \(A/P\) reference point is year 2 — exactly where the grown principal now sits. Apply directly:

$$A = 501760(A/P,12\%,25) = 501760 \times 0.1275$$

Answer

\(A = \) Rs. 63,974 per year

Compare with Q9

Skipping two years raises each instalment from Rs. 51,000 to Rs. 63,974 — a 25% increase, applied to all 25 payments. Total extra cost: about Rs. 3.24 lakh, on a Rs. 4 lakh loan, purely for a two-year grace period. Deferral compounds against you.

3.5 · The five-equivalents problem

Multi · Q11 — Texas drilling rights

A design-build-operate engineering company in Texas plans to lease drilling rights to a mining company. The contract calls for the mining company to pay $20,000 per year for 20 years starting 3 years from now (end of year 3 through year 22), plus $10,000 six years from now and $15,000 sixteen years from now. At 16% per year, determine: (1) total present worth \(P\) in year 0; (2) future worth \(F\) in year 22; (3) annual series over all 22 years; (4) annual series over the first 10 years; (5) annual series over the last 12 years.

Why this is the perfect final problem. It takes one cash flow and asks for five different equivalent representations. Once you have \(P_0\), every other answer is a single factor away. Solve for \(P_0\) once, then harvest.

All flows are receipts to the landowner. The two taller arrows are the one-off payments at years 6 and 16.

(1) Present worth at year 0

The series runs years 3–22, so \(n = 22-3+1 = 20\) payments, and \((P/A,16\%,20)\) lands at year 2 — hence the extra \((P/F,16\%,2)\):

$$P_0 = 20000(P/A,16\%,20)(P/F,16\%,2) + 10000(P/F,16\%,6) + 15000(P/F,16\%,16)$$

$$= 20000(5.9288)(0.7432) + 10000(0.4104) + 15000(0.0930)$$

$$= 88{,}125.7 + 4{,}104 + 1{,}395$$

Answer (1)

\(P_0 = \) $93,624.68

(2) Future worth at year 22

$$F_{22} = P_0(F/P,16\%,22) = 93624.68 \times 26.1864$$

Answer (2)

\(F_{22} = \) $24,51,693

(3) Annual series over all 22 years

$$A_{1\text{–}22} = P_0(A/P,16\%,22) = 93624.68 \times 0.1664$$

Answer (3)

\(A = \) $15,579 per year for years 1–22

(4) Annual series over the first 10 years

Same \(P_0\), but now spread over only 10 years — so each payment must be larger:

$$A_{1\text{–}10} = P_0(A/P,16\%,10) = 93624.68 \times 0.2069$$

Answer (4)

\(A = \) $19,371 per year for years 1–10

(5) Annual series over the last 12 years

This one requires thought about which anchor to use. The last 12 years are years 11–22, and that block ends at year 22 — so anchor on \(F_{22}\), not \(P_0\), and use \(A/F\):

$$A_{11\text{–}22} = F_{22}(A/F,16\%,12) = 2451693 \times 0.0324$$

Answer (5)

\(A = \) $79,435 per year for years 11–22

Read what these five numbers are telling you

All five describe the same lease. $93,625 today ≡ $2.45 million in year 22 ≡ $15,579/yr for 22 years ≡ $19,371/yr for 10 years ≡ $79,435/yr for the last 12. Notice how much larger the last-12-years figure is: money promised late is heavily discounted at 16%, so recovering the same value in a compressed, distant window demands far bigger payments.

The exam technique: when a question asks for several equivalents of one cash flow, compute \(P_0\) properly once, then use single factors. Never rebuild the cash flow from scratch five times — that is five chances to make an error, and about four times the work.

Extra · Q1 — Alex's property purchase

Alex is considering buying a property comprising a plot of land and a shop. The land's value after five years is ₹30,00,000 and the shop has no salvage value. The shop can be rented out immediately for a net annual revenue of ₹3,50,000. Annual maintenance is estimated to start at ₹50,000 in the second year and grow by ₹2,000 annually. If Alex wants a 20% return, how much can he pay for this property now?

Set up the equivalence. Alex should pay exactly what the property is worth to him: \(PW(\text{Income}) = PW(\text{Costs})\). Income has two pieces — the land's resale value (a single future sum) and the rental revenue (a uniform series). Costs are the purchase price \(P\) now, plus a maintenance series that doesn't start until year 2 — a deferred gradient.

Rental income every year 1–5, land value released at year 5, maintenance a deferred gradient starting year 2.

PW of income — land value (single sum, year 5) plus rent (ordinary annuity, years 1–5):

$$PW(\text{Income}) = 30{,}00{,}000(P/F,20\%,5) + 3{,}50{,}000(P/A,20\%,5) = 30{,}00{,}000(0.4019) + 3{,}50{,}000(2.991)$$

$$= 12{,}05{,}700 + 10{,}46{,}850 = 22{,}52{,}550$$

(Using the standard table factor \((P/A,20\%,5)=2.991\); your slide used 2.911, which looks like a transcription slip — 2.991 is the textbook value. This changes the final answer slightly from the slide's figure, shown below.)

PW of costs — maintenance is ₹50,000 at year 2, growing by ₹2,000/year for 4 years (years 2–5, so \(n=4\) locally). Convert the gradient to an equivalent uniform value, bring it to year 1 with \((P/A)\), then to year 0 with one more \((P/F)\):

$$PW(\text{Costs}) = P + \big[50{,}000 + 2000(A/G,20\%,4)\big](P/A,20\%,4)(P/F,20\%,1)$$

Section 4Nominal & Effective Interest Rate

4.1 · Why "9% per year" can secretly mean two different things

Every formula in Sections 2 and 3 quietly assumed one thing: that the interest rate you were given compounds exactly as often as your cash flows occur — an annual rate, applied once a year, to yearly cash flows. Real interest rates break that assumption constantly. A savings account might advertise "9% per year" but actually add interest four times a year, a quarter of that rate each time. Does compounding it in smaller, more frequent chunks change what you actually earn in a year? Yes — and this section is entirely about that gap.

The two rates, defined
  • Nominal interest rate (r) — the stated annual rate, ignoring the effect of compounding frequency. Also called the Annual Percentage Rate (APR). By itself it tells you almost nothing about what you'll actually earn.
  • Effective interest rate (ieff) — the true rate you earn (or pay) over a year, once compounding is accounted for. Also called the Annual Percentage Yield (APY). This is the number that actually determines how much money changes hands.

The rule that makes this whole section work: every nominal rate must be converted into an effective rate before it goes anywhere near a P/A, F/P, or any other factor. The seven formulas from Section 2 only ever want an effective rate.

The insight

Earning 2.25% four times a year is equivalent to earning 9.3083% just once a year — not 9%. The extra 0.3083 percentage points is "interest on interest": by the second quarter, you're earning 2.25% on a balance that already includes the first quarter's interest. The effective rate is always \(\geq\) the nominal rate, and the gap widens as compounding gets more frequent.

The effective interest rate formula

Generalising the warm-up above — let \(m\) = number of compounding periods per year, \(r\) = nominal interest rate, and \(n\) = number of compounding periods per payment period (for annual cash flows, \(n=m\); \(n=1\) only when the payment period and the compounding period coincide):

$$i_{eff} = \left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right)^{n} - 1$$

When you just want the plain effective annual rate (payment period = 1 year), \(n=m\), and this simplifies to the more familiar

$$i_{eff,\,\text{annual}} = \left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right)^{m} - 1$$

4.3 · Reading interest-rate statements correctly

4.4 · The general formula — when payment period ≠ compounding period

So far every example compounded and paid interest on the same schedule. But real problems often mix schedules — monthly deposits into an account that compounds quarterly, for instance. The full formula handles this by explicitly separating three ideas:

  • CP (Compounding Period) — how often the bank actually adds interest.
  • PP (Payment Period) — how often your cash flows (deposits/withdrawals) occur.
  • m — number of compounding periods per year.

The exponent itself needs one more piece of care: CP and PP name time spans (a quarter, a month), so writing the exponent as a bare "CP/PP" invites reading it as one duration divided by another, backwards from what's intended. Define it explicitly instead:

$$k = \frac{\text{compounding periods per year}}{\text{payment periods per year}} = \frac{\text{duration of one PP}}{\text{duration of one CP}}$$ $$i_{eff \text{ per PP}} = \left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right)^{k} - 1$$

Read \(k\) as "how many compounding periods fit inside one payment period." If deposits are quarterly but compounding is monthly, three compounding periods sit inside each payment period, so \(k=3\).

4.6 · When your cash flows don't match the compounding period

The trickiest — and most common — real-world case: you deposit money on one schedule (say, monthly), but the bank compounds on a different schedule (say, quarterly). The fix is always the same: convert to the effective rate for your payment period, using \(k\) as the exponent, then use the ordinary formulas exactly as before.

Nominal/Effective · Q17 — three rate zones on one timeline

A cash flow diagram shows a withdrawal of $15,000 at year 1 and withdrawals of $2,000 at years 3, 4, 5. The interest rate is 16% compounded quarterly for years 0–2, 9% per year for years 2–3, and 15% per year for years 3–5. Determine the present value.

The governing idea: discount each cash flow only through the rate zone(s) it actually passes through, moving one zone at a time. Never apply one blended rate across a boundary where the rate genuinely changes.

Step 1 — the $15,000 at year 1 sits entirely inside the "years 0–2" zone. Convert 16% compounded quarterly into an effective annual rate for that zone, then discount one year directly:

$$= 6945 \times 0.7561 \times 0.9174 \times 0.7306 = \$3{,}519.6$$

Answer

\(P = 12{,}821.6+3{,}519.6 = \) $16,341.2

Interest compounds multiplicatively, not additively — a flow that crosses a 16.99% zone and then a 9% zone is worth \((1.1699)(1.09)\), not \((1+\frac{16.99+9}{2})\). Always walk a cash flow through its zones one at a time, in order, exactly as done above.

5.1 · Comparing alternatives — the vocabulary

Everything so far in this guide has answered "is this one project worth doing?" Real decisions are usually a choice between options — which vendor, which machine, which financing plan. 5.2 · The Present Worth criterion, and its two sign conventions

The idea is simple: reduce every alternative's entire cash flow to a single number — its value at time 0 — using an interest rate that represents the return the firm requires (the Minimum Attractive Rate of Return, or MARR). Whichever number wins depends on how you've signed the cash flows, and your slides give two equally valid conventions:

Cost-dominated CFD

Costs (outflows) are positive; revenues, profits, and salvage value (inflows) are negative. \(PW=P+\sum C_j(P/F,i,j)-S(P/F,i,n)\). Lower PW is better — you're minimising a cost.

Revenue-dominated CFD

Revenue, profit, and salvage (inflows) are positive; costs (outflows) are negative. \(PW=-P+\sum R_j(P/F,i,j)+S(P/F,i,n)\). Higher PW is better — you're maximising net worth.

Both conventions describe the exact same economics — pick whichever framing makes a given problem easiest to set up. Most problems below are naturally cost-dominated (comparing purchase/running costs of equipment); a few are naturally revenue-dominated (comparing business ventures with income streams).

The Present Worth criterion, step by step
  1. Determine the MARR — the interest rate the firm wants to earn on its investment.
  2. Estimate the service life of each alternative.
  3. Determine the net cash flow of each alternative (inflows − outflows) at every point in time.
  4. Compute PW at the MARR for each alternative.
  5. Select: for a single project, accept if \(PW>0\) (revenue-dominated) or reject if \(PW\) is too costly; for mutually exclusive projects, pick the one with the highest revenue-dominated PW, or the lowest cost-dominated PW.

5.4 · When you're choosing a combination, not just one option

Real capital budgeting rarely offers a single mutually-exclusive choice — usually you have a fixed pot of money and several projects competing for it, and you can fund any combination that fits the budget. The method doesn't change: list every combination that fits within budget, find each one's total PW, and pick the winner.

It's tempting to assume a "which combination is best" question always has a genuinely profitable answer. 5.5 · Present worth with unequal lives

Every problem so far compared alternatives with the same life. What if one machine lasts 5 years and its competitor lasts 8? Comparing their raw present worths directly is meaningless — the 8-year machine's PW covers three more years of service that the 5-year machine's PW doesn't even attempt to. Your slides give two standard fixes.

1. Common Multiple (repeated-project) method

Extend the comparison to the Least Common Multiple (LCM) of both lives, assuming each alternative is simply repeated (bought again, identically) as many times as needed to fill that span. A 3-year and a 4-year asset are compared over 12 years — the 3-year one repeated 4 times, the 4-year one repeated 3 times.

2. Study Period method

Pick a fixed analysis period that makes business sense (often shorter than either asset's actual life), and estimate each asset's market/salvage value at that cutoff point, even mid-life. No repetition or replacement is assumed — you simply value what you'd have, and what you could sell it for, at the study period's end.

Why the two methods can disagree

They're answering genuinely different questions — "which is cheaper if I keep replacing forever" vs. "which is cheaper if I only care about the next \(X\) years." It's entirely possible, and worth expecting, for the two methods to recommend different alternatives on the very same data. Several problems below show exactly this.

5.8 · Capitalized Cost — present worth when a project never ends

Every Present Worth problem so far has had a finite life — even the "unequal lives" problems in 5.5–5.6 eventually stopped, at an LCM or a study period. Some projects genuinely don't: bridges, dams, highways, hydroelectric plants, permanent endowments. Their planning horizon is, for all practical purposes, infinite. This subsection is Present Worth's answer to that case.

Definition — Capitalized Cost

Capitalized Cost (CC) is the present worth of a project with a very long (35–40+ year) or infinite life. It's the single lump sum that, invested today at rate \(i\), would generate enough interest each period to fund the project's costs forever, without the principal ever being drawn down.

Deriving the formula — let n walk off to infinity

Start from the ordinary \((P/A)\) relation, \(P=A(P/A,i,n)=A\left[\dfrac{(1+i)^n-1}{i(1+i)^n}\right]\). As \(n\to\infty\), the ratio inside the brackets simplifies dramatically — dividing every term by \((1+i)^n\):

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(1+i)^n-1}{i(1+i)^n} = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1-(1+i)^{-n}}{i} = \frac{1-0}{i} = \frac{1}{i}$$

So as \(n\to\infty\), \(P\to A/i\). We rename \(P\) to \(CC\) as a reminder that this is specifically a capitalized-cost equivalence (and \(A\) can equally be called \(AW\), annual worth):

$$CC = \frac{A}{i} = \frac{AW}{i}$$

Analogy

Think of a scholarship endowment. If a university wants to hand out $10,000 every year forever, and can earn 5% on invested funds, it needs exactly $10,000/0.05 = $200,000 sitting in the fund today. Each year, that $200,000 earns exactly $10,000 in interest — hand that out, and the $200,000 principal is untouched, ready to earn the same $10,000 again next year. That's capitalized cost in one sentence: the principal that sustains itself forever at a given payout rate.

Adding a first cost: the general CC formula

If \(P\) is the first cost of the asset itself, and \(A\) is the uniform annual disbursement (or the uniform difference between annual receipts and disbursements, for a revenue-generating asset):

$$CC = P + A(P/A,i,\infty) = P+\frac{A}{i}$$

When there's no revenue — a pure-cost public project — this is simply \(CC=P+\dfrac{\text{Annual worth of costs}}{i}\).

Comparing infinite-life alternatives

Find the CC of each alternative using the procedure above, and compare directly — the alternative with the smaller capitalized cost is the more economical one. Because CC already represents the total present worth of financing and maintaining an alternative forever, every comparison is automatically over the same (infinite) horizon — none of Section 5.5's LCM or study-period headaches apply here.

The five-step method

  1. Draw a cash flow diagram showing all non-recurring and recurring cash flows, ideally for two life cycles (to make the repeating pattern visually clear).
  2. Find the present worth of all non-recurring amounts.
  3. Find the equivalent uniform annual worth (\(AW\)) through one life cycle of all recurring amounts.
  4. Divide the \(AW\) from step 3 by \(i\) — this converts it into a capitalized cost.
  5. Add the results of steps 2 and 4.

Every CC problem below is built from exactly two ingredients, added together: (i) ordinary present-worth pieces for anything that happens only once, and (ii) \(\dfrac{AW}{i}\) for anything that repeats forever — where \(AW\) is itself found using whatever Section 2 factor turns that repeating pattern into a uniform annual amount over one cycle (\((A/F)\) for a periodic lump sum, \((A/G)\) for a gradient, or just the amount itself if it's already annual).

Alternatives A and B aren't stated as perpetual patterns directly — they're ordinary finite cash flows that are then assumed to repeat forever. The reliable route is always: find the ordinary present worth of one cycle (\(P\)), convert to that cycle's equivalent annual worth (\(AW\)) with \((A/P)\), and only then divide by \(i\) to capitalize. Skipping straight to \(CC=P/i\) without the \((A/P)\) step is a common error — it only works when the "P" is already a perpetuity's own principal, not a finite-life cash flow's present worth.

Running the formula backward, and perpetuities that don't start on day one

Two variations round out the toolkit: sometimes you're given the capitalized cost and asked to find a missing piece (usually the first cost); sometimes the perpetual payments don't begin until several years in. Capitalized Cost · Q13 — solving backward for the first cost

A music academy receives a gift of Rs. 5,00,000 for construction and perpetual upkeep of a music shell. Annual maintenance is Rs. 15,000; additionally, Rs. 25,000 is needed every 10 years for repairs. Funds earn 6% (tax-free). How much of the gift is left over for the initial construction cost, after perpetual upkeep is funded?

The gift must cover BOTH the (unknown) construction cost AND perpetual upkeep — solve backward for what's left for construction.

Rearrange the ordinary CC relation to solve for the unknown piece — here, the "first cost" is what's left over, not what's given:

$$CC = \text{First cost} + \frac{A}{i} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{First cost} = CC - \frac{A}{i}$$

where \(A\) is the total annual-equivalent disbursement: the flat Rs. 15,000 maintenance, plus the Rs. 25,000-every-10-years repair converted to its annual equivalent via \((A/F)\):

$$A = 15{,}000+25{,}000(A/F,6\%,10) = 15{,}000+25{,}000(0.0759) = \text{Rs. }16{,}896.70$$

$$\text{First cost} = 5{,}00{,}000-\frac{16{,}896.70}{0.06}$$

Answer

Available for construction: Rs. 2,18,388.35 (matches your slide's Rs. 2,18,387.50 to rounding) — the rest of the Rs. 5,00,000 gift (≈Rs. 2,81,612) stays invested to fund maintenance forever.

Capitalized Cost · Q14 — an investor's deferred perpetual withdrawal

An investor earns 15%/year. He invests $10,000 now, $30,000 three years from now, and $8,000/year for 5 years starting 4 years from now. How much can he withdraw every year forever, beginning 12 years from now?

Three deposits accumulate first; only from year 12 does the perpetual withdrawal begin.

This is a perpetuity problem in reverse gear — instead of finding the lump sum needed to fund a perpetual payment, we're told the deposits and must find the perpetual payment they support. First, grow every deposit forward to year 12 (the moment withdrawals begin):

$$F_{12} = 10{,}000(F/P,15\%,12)+30{,}000(F/P,15\%,9)+8{,}000(F/A,15\%,5)(F/P,15\%,3)$$

(the 5-year, $8,000/yr deposit series runs years 4–8; its \((F/A)\) value lands at year 8, then grows 3 more years to reach year 12)

$$= 10{,}000(5.3503)+30{,}000(3.5179)+8{,}000(6.7424)(1.5209) = \$2{,}41{,}073$$

Now flip the usual CC logic: a lump sum \(F_{12}\) sitting at year 12 (one period before the first withdrawal) supports a perpetual annual withdrawal of \(A=F_{12}\times i\) — the exact reverse of \(CC=A/i\):

$$A = 2{,}41{,}073\times0.15$$

Answer

He can withdraw ≈$36,161 every year, forever, starting in year 12.

Capitalized Cost · Q15 — Sarah Bishop's deferred scholarship endowment

Sarah wants to fund $10,000/year scholarships for 4 students, beginning in year 6 and continuing indefinitely. At 10%/year, how much must she donate now?

Scholarships begin in year 6, not year 1 — a deferred perpetuity.

Total scholarship outlay is \(4\times\$10{,}000=\$40{,}000\)/year, forever, but not starting until year 6. The shortcut \(CC=A/i\) always values a perpetuity one period before its first payment — so applying it directly gives the value at year 5, not year 0:

$$P_5 = \frac{40{,}000}{i} = \frac{40{,}000}{0.10} = \$4{,}00{,}000$$

Now discount that lump sum back the remaining 5 years to today:

$$P_0 = 4{,}00{,}000(P/F,10\%,5) = 4{,}00{,}000(0.6209)$$

Answer

Sarah must donate ≈$248,369 now.

The one habit that prevents every deferred-perpetuity mistake

\(A/i\) always lands one period before the first payment — never at year 0 unless the first payment genuinely falls at year 1. Q13's shell and Q1–Q12's "forever from year X" pieces all quietly used this same fact (dividing by \(i\) to get a value at the year before the perpetuity starts, then discounting that lump sum back to year 0 with an ordinary \((P/F)\)). Whenever a perpetuity is delayed, that extra \((P/F)\) step is not optional.

What Section 5.8 adds to your toolkit

Capitalized cost is nothing more than the \((P/A)\) factor pushed to its \(n\to\infty\) limit, giving the clean shortcut \(CC=A/i\). Every problem above is built from the same short checklist: separate non-recurring pieces (ordinary present worth) from recurring-forever pieces (\(AW/i\)); if a repeating pattern isn't already a flat annual amount, convert one cycle of it to an equivalent \(AW\) first with \((A/F)\), \((A/G)\), or \((A/P)\); and if a perpetuity doesn't start at year 1, remember \(A/i\) lands one period before its first payment — always chase that extra \((P/F)\) back to year 0.

Section 6Future Worth Method

6.1 · The Future Worth criterion

Present Worth asks "what is this alternative worth today?" Future Worth asks the mirror-image question: "what will this alternative be worth at the end of its life?" Same underlying economics, same seven formulas — just every cash flow is rolled forward to a common future date instead of discounted back to today. Whichever alternative you'd pick by PW, you'll pick by FW too — but FW is often the more natural framing when a decision is genuinely about "what will I have at the end," e.g. retirement planning or a fixed-term contract.

Revenue-dominated CFD

Revenue and salvage positive, costs negative:

$$FW(i) = -P(1+i)^n + R_1(1+i)^{n-1}+\dots+R_n+S$$

Higher FW wins.

Cost-dominated CFD

Costs positive, revenue and salvage negative:

$$FW(i) = P(1+i)^n + C_1(1+i)^{n-1}+\dots+C_n-S$$

Lower FW wins.

In practice, every term above is just \(P(F/P,i,n)\), \(A(F/A,i,n)\), or a single amount left untouched (if it already sits at year \(n\)) — exactly the factors from Section 2, aimed at the end of the timeline instead of the start.

What Section 6 adds to your toolkit

Future Worth is Present Worth's mirror image — same sign conventions, same seven formulas, same care needed around unequal lives and annuity-due timing (Q8's lease is a perfect example of Section 1's end-of-period convention mattering again). It's the same arithmetic, but organising it this way catches timing errors (like Q8's lease) far more reliably.

Section 7Annual Worth Method

7.1 · What is Annual Worth?

Present Worth collapses a whole cash flow down to a single value today. Future Worth collapses it down to a single value at the end. Annual Worth does the third natural thing: it spreads everything into a single, equivalent, uniform amount recurring every year of the alternative's life.

Other names for the same idea

Annual Worth (AW) is also known as: equivalent annual worth (EAW), equivalent annual cost (EAC), annual equivalent (AE), and equivalent uniform annual cost (EUAC). All five names describe exactly the same computation — expect any of them to show up in a problem statement.

AW, PW, and FW are three views of the same number

$$AW = PW(A/P,i,n) = FW(A/F,i,n)$$

Since all three are simply equivalent restatements of one another, the alternative selected by the AW method will always agree with the alternative selected by the PW or FW method — provided all three are carried out correctly. Nothing new is being decided here; AW is simply a more convenient lens for certain problems, as the next section shows.

7.2 · Why AW is the easiest method when lives are unequal

Recall from Section 5.5 that comparing Present Worths across unequal-life alternatives forces a choice: extend everything to the LCM of the lives (assuming repeated purchases), or fix a study period and estimate market values at that cutoff. Annual Worth sidesteps this entirely.

The key computational advantage

The \(n\) in the AW factors is the number of years for equal-service comparison — normally the LCM, or a stated study period, exactly as in the PW method. But once cash flows are converted to an AW value, that value applies to every year of the life cycle, and to every future life cycle identically. The AW computed over just one life cycle already is the AW for the LCM, for two life cycles, for ten — for as long as the pattern keeps repeating.

Therefore, it is never necessary to find the LCM of the lives to satisfy the equal-service requirement when using AW.

Why this works — the intuition

Think of AW as "the flat annual rent this alternative effectively costs (or earns) forever, at this rate." If a machine and its exact replacement cost you a flat $10,000/year in perpetuity, it makes no difference whether you're comparing 4 years of it or 40 — the flat annual figure is identical either way. Contrast this with PW, where the total lump sum obviously depends on how many years you tally up.

7.3 · Capital Recovery — the two-part structure of AW

The pieces
  • P — initial investment / first cost: the total first cost of all assets and services required to initiate the alternative.
  • S — salvage value at the end of the useful life.
  • A — the annual amount (annual operating cost, AOC, or M&O cost).

The AW value for an alternative is comprised of two components: capital recovery for the initial investment \(P\) at a stated interest rate (usually the MARR), and the equivalent annual amount \(A\). The symbol \(CR\) denotes the capital recovery component:

$$AW = CR + A$$

Deriving the CR formula

The \((A/P)\) factor converts \(P\) into an equivalent annual cost. If the asset has a positive salvage value \(S\) at the end of its life, that value's equivalent annual worth — recovered via the \((A/F)\) factor — reduces the equivalent annual cost. So:

$$CR = P(A/P,i,n) - S(A/F,i,n)$$

There is a standard relation between the \((A/P)\) and \((A/F)\) factors (both recover the same principal, but one accounts for interest along the way and the other doesn't):

$$(A/F,i,n) = (A/P,i,n) - i$$

Substitute this into the CR equation to eliminate \((A/F)\) entirely:

$$CR = P(A/P,i,n) - S\big[(A/P,i,n)-i\big] = (P-S)(A/P,i,n) + Si$$

The working formula used throughout this section

$$CR = (P-S)(A/P,i,n) + S(i)$$

7.4 · Selection guidelines

The Annual Worth method is typically the easiest to apply of all the evaluation techniques once the MARR is specified — each alternative's AW is computed over its own life, and the guidelines mirror the PW method exactly:

  • One alternative: if \(AW\ge0\), the MARR is met or exceeded — the alternative is economically justified.
  • Two or more (mutually exclusive) alternatives: select the alternative whose AW is numerically largest — i.e. least negative or most positive. For cost-dominated alternatives this means the lowest AW of cost; for revenue-dominated alternatives it means the largest AW of net cash flow.

What Section 7 adds to your toolkit

Annual Worth is Present Worth and Future Worth's third sibling — same underlying equivalence, same seven factors, same selection rule (largest AW wins) — but with one genuine practical advantage: because an AW value computed over one life cycle automatically holds for every future cycle, unequal-life comparisons never require an LCM or a study period. The relationships between the seven

There are really only four independent ideas; the rest are reciprocals.

  • \((F/P) \times (P/F) = 1\) — inverse pair (Formulas 1 & 2)
  • \((F/A) \times (A/F) = 1\) — inverse pair (Formulas 3 & 4)
  • \((P/A) \times (A/P) = 1\) — inverse pair (Formulas 5 & 6)
  • \((P/A) = (F/A) \times (P/F)\) — the chain that derives Formula 5 from 3 and 2
  • \((A/P) = (A/F) + i\) — a neat identity worth knowing: the instalment equals the sinking-fund deposit plus the interest on the principal

Exam traps — the ten that actually cost marks

Check every one of these before you hand in the paper
  1. \(P/A\) positioning. It lands one period before the first payment. If a series starts at year 5, the \(P/A\) value sits at year 4.
  2. \(F/A\) positioning. It lands at the last payment, not one period after.
  3. Counting payments. A series from year \(a\) to year \(b\) has \(b - a + 1\) payments. Years 3 to 22 is 20, not 19.
  4. "Beginning of year \(k\)" = end of year \(k-1\). Shift by one and state your interpretation.
  5. Travel distance for single flows. \(n = \) target year − flow's year. Never the problem's total horizon.
  6. Unit consistency. Monthly payments need a monthly \(i\) and \(n\) in months.
  7. Interest vs amount. "The interest is Rs. 51" ≠ "the amount is Rs. 51". Add the principal first.
  8. Gradient \(n\). Use the number of cash flows in the gradient, not the length of the story. Four maintenance payments means \(n=4\), even if the question mentions six years.
  9. \(A/G\) is never the final answer. It produces a uniform series; you still need \(P/A\) or \(F/A\).
  10. Netting simultaneous flows. If revenue and cost occur in the same years, net them before choosing a factor.
Answer-writing checklist for full marks
  1. Draw the cash flow diagram with year numbers, arrows and \(i\) marked. Often carries marks by itself.
  2. List the given data — \(P, F, A, G, i, n\) — and state which is unknown.
  3. Write the factor notation first (e.g. \(P = A(P/A, 15\%, 5)(P/F, 15\%, 2)\)) before substituting numbers. If your arithmetic slips, the method still earns marks.
  4. Substitute table values explicitly so the examiner can follow.
  5. Box the answer with its unit — "Rs. 8,200 per year", not a bare number.
  6. Add one sentence of interpretation where the question invites it.
