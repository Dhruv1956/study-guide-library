Present Worth of a Single Future Amount

How much must be invested now at 16% compounded annually so that $1,811 can be received 4 years hence?

Given
  • Future amount, F = $1,811
  • Interest rate, i = 16% per year, compounded annually
  • Number of periods, n = 4 years
  • Find: Present amount, P
Cash Flow Diagram
Step-by-Step Solution
  1. Identify the problem type. We are given a single future amount and asked for its equivalent value today — this is a single-payment present-worth problem. The governing relationship connects P and F through the compound-interest factor \((P/F, i, n)\), which answers: "what is $1 received n years from now worth today, if money grows at rate i?"
  2. Write the working equation. \[ P = F(P/F,\,i,\,n) = \dfrac{F}{(1+i)^n} \]
  3. Compute the compounding factor. \[ (1+i)^n = (1.16)^4 = 1.8106 \] \[ (P/F,16\%,4) = \dfrac{1}{(1.16)^4} = \dfrac{1}{1.8106} = 0.55229 \]
  4. Substitute and solve. \[ P = 1811 \times 0.55229 = \$1,000.20 \]
Final Answer\(P \approx \$1,000.20. Investing about $1,000.20 today, left to grow at 16% per year, will accumulate to exactly \$1,811 in 4 years.\)
Q2

Finding the Rate of Return from P and F

You buy a share of stock for \$20 and sell it for \$40. If this happens within a year, i = 100%. If it takes 5 years, what is the rate of return on your investment?

Given
  • Present (purchase) amount, P = \$20
  • Future (sale) amount, F = \$40
  • Number of years, n = 5
  • Find: interest rate i such that P grows to F in n years
Cash Flow Diagram
Step-by-Step Solution
  1. Set up the single-payment compound-amount relation. Money doubles from \$20 to \$40, but this time it is stretched over 5 years instead of 1 — so the *annual* growth rate must be lower than the 100% one-year figure. We use: \[ F = P(1+i)^n \]
  2. Substitute known values. \[ 40 = 20(1+i)^5 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (1+i)^5 = 2 \]
  3. Solve for i by taking the 5th root of both sides. \[ 1+i = 2^{1/5} = 1.148698 \] \[ i = 2^{1/5} - 1 = 0.148698 \]
Final Answer\(i \approx 14.87\%\text{ per year}. This confirms an important TVM idea: doubling your money in 1 year needs 100% annual growth, but doubling it over 5 years needs only about 14.9% annual growth — compounding does the heavy lifting.\)
Q3

Future Worth of Deposits Made Every Two Years

“Now” is June 30, 1994. Three payments of \$500 each are to be received every 2 years, starting 2 years from now, and deposited where they earn 7% per year. How large will the account be on June 30, 2002?

Given
  • Deposit amount = \$500, made at year 2, year 4, and year 6
  • Interest rate, i = 7% per year, compounded annually
  • Target date = June 30, 2002 = year 8 (8 years after “now”)
  • Find: F at year 8
Cash Flow Diagram
Step-by-Step Solution
  1. Map the timeline. 1994 is t = 0. Deposits fall on t = 2, t = 4, and t = 6 (every 2 years, starting 2 years from now). The valuation date, 2002, is t = 8. Because each deposit sits alone (not a simple annual annuity, since the spacing is 2 years, not 1), the cleanest approach is to move each deposit individually to t = 8 using the single-payment compound-amount factor \((F/P, i, n)\), then add them up — this is just the superposition principle for cash flows.
  2. Write the working equation. Each \$500 grows at 7%/year for however many years remain until 2002: \[ F = 500(F/P,7\%,6) + 500(F/P,7\%,4) + 500(F/P,7\%,2) \] (6 years of growth for the t=2 deposit, 4 years for the t=4 deposit, 2 years for the t=6 deposit.)
  3. Compute each factor. \[ (1.07)^2 = 1.14490, \qquad (1.07)^4 = 1.31080, \qquad (1.07)^6 = 1.50073 \]
  4. Substitute and add. \[ F = 500(1.50073) + 500(1.31080) + 500(1.14490) \] \[ F = 750.37 + 655.40 + 572.45 = \$1,978.21 \]
  5. Cross-check with an alternative route (optional, but good practice). Convert the 7%/year rate into an effective 2-year rate, \(i_2 = (1.07)^2 - 1 = 14.4900\%\), treat the 3 deposits as an ordinary annuity of "period" length 2 years, find its value at the time of the last deposit using \((F/A, i_2, 3)\), then roll forward 2 more years to reach 2002: \[ F = 500(F/A,\,i_2,\,3)\times(1+i_2) = \$1,978.21 \] Same answer — confirming our arithmetic.
Final Answer\(F \approx \$1,978.21\)

Finding n — How Long Until an Investment Doubles

You purchase 100 shares of Citigroup stock at \$60/share. You will sell when the market price doubles. If you expect the price to increase 20% per year, how long do you anticipate waiting?

Given
  • Present value (per share), P = \$60
  • Target future value, F = 2P = \$120
  • Growth rate, i = 20% per year
  • Find: n (number of years)
Cash Flow Diagram
Step-by-Step Solution
  1. Recognize the structure. The number of shares (100) is irrelevant to how long the doubling takes \u2014 doubling is a ratio, so we can work with a single share: \$60 must grow to \$120.
  2. Start from the compound-amount relation and isolate n. \[ F = P(1+i)^n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dfrac{F}{P} = (1+i)^n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2 = (1.2)^n \]
  3. Solve for n by taking logarithms of both sides (any base works, since it cancels): \[ \ln 2 = n\ln 1.2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n = \dfrac{\ln 2}{\ln 1.2} \]
  4. Compute. \[ n = \dfrac{0.69315}{0.18232} = 3.802\text{ years} \] This is the well-known "Rule of 72" territory \u2014 as a quick sanity check, \(72/20 \approx 3.6\) years, close to our exact 3.80.
Final Answer\(n \approx 3.80\text{ years}\ (\approx 3\text{ years and }293\text{ days}).\)

Present Worth of an Irregular Series of Withdrawals

Wilson Technology wishes to set aside money now to cover expenses over the next 4 years: \$25,000 (year 1), \$3,000 (year 2), nothing (year 3), \$5,000 (year 4). At 10% interest, how much must be deposited now?

Given
  • Year 1 withdrawal = \$25,000
  • Year 2 withdrawal = \$3,000
  • Year 3 withdrawal = \$0
  • Year 4 withdrawal = \$5,000
  • Interest rate, i = 10% per year
  • Find: P (deposit needed now)
Cash Flow Diagram
Step-by-Step Solution
  1. Recognize the structure. These four withdrawals are irregular \u2014 they don't form a uniform series or a clean gradient, so we treat each one as an independent single future amount and discount it back to t = 0 separately. This is the most fundamental TVM technique: any cash-flow diagram, however messy, can always be solved amount-by-amount using \((P/F,i,n)\).
  2. Write the working equation (year 3 contributes nothing, so it drops out): \[ P = 25000(P/F,10\%,1) + 3000(P/F,10\%,2) + 5000(P/F,10\%,4) \]
  3. Compute each discount factor. \[ (P/F,10\%,1)=0.9091, \quad (P/F,10\%,2)=0.8264, \quad (P/F,10\%,4)=0.6830 \]
  4. Substitute and add. \[ P = 25000(0.9091) + 3000(0.8264) + 5000(0.6830) \] \[ P = 22,727.27 + 2,479.34 + 3,415.07 = \$28,621.68 \]
Final Answer\(P \approx \$28,621.68\)
Q6

Combined Savings Plan — Deposits and Withdrawals Together

A 10-year savings plan: deposit \$600 today, \$800 at the end of each of the next 5 years, and \$2,000 at the end of each of the remaining 5 years. Withdraw \$300 at the end of each of the first 3 years, and \$350 each year thereafter through year 10. In addition, withdraw \$50 every 2 years through year 15. i = 12%. (i) Draw the CFD. (ii) Find the amount accumulated at the end of year 15.

Given
  • Deposits: \$600 (t=0), \$800/yr (t=1–5), \$2,000/yr (t=6–10)
  • Withdrawals: \$300/yr (t=1–3), \$350/yr (t=4–10)
  • Extra withdrawal: \$50 every 2 years, t = 2,4,6,8,10,12,14
  • i = 12% per year, compounded annually
  • Find: F at t = 15
Cash Flow Diagram
Step-by-Step Solution
  1. Part (i) \u2014 build the diagram. When several cash-flow streams land on the same date, the standard first move in any engineering-economy problem is to combine everything into one net cash flow per period (deposits count negative \u2014 money leaving your hand \u2014 and withdrawals count positive \u2014 money coming back to you). For example at t = 2: an \$800 deposit, a \$300 scheduled withdrawal, and the \$50 extra withdrawal all coincide, netting to \u2212800+300+50 = \u2212\$450. The diagram above shows this consolidated, single-arrow-per-year view \u2014 exactly what you'd hand in as "the CFD."
  2. Part (ii) \u2014 strategy. Rather than accumulate 16 individual net values, it's far more reliable to treat the problem as six separate uniform/derived series, move each to t = 15 with the single-payment factor \((F/P,i,n)\), and sum them (superposition again \u2014 the same idea as Q3, just with annuities instead of single payments this time).
  3. Series 1 \u2014 the \$600 lump sum today. Grows for the full 15 years: \[ 600(F/P,12\%,15) = 600(5.4736) = \$3,284.14 \]
  4. Series 2 \u2014 \$800/yr deposits, t = 1 to 5. First find their value at t = 5 with \((F/A,12\%,5)\), then roll that lump forward 10 more years to t = 15 with \((F/P,12\%,10)\): \[ 800(F/A,12\%,5)(F/P,12\%,10) = 800(6.3528)(3.1058) = \$15,784.78 \]
  5. Series 3 \u2014 \$2,000/yr deposits, t = 6 to 10. Value at t = 10 via \((F/A,12\%,5)\), rolled forward 5 more years: \[ 2000(F/A,12\%,5)(F/P,12\%,5) = 2000(6.3528)(1.7623) = \$22,391.78 \]
  6. Series 4 \u2014 \$300/yr withdrawals, t = 1 to 3. Value at t = 3, rolled forward 12 years (subtracted, since it's money coming back out of the growing balance): \[ -300(F/A,12\%,3)(F/P,12\%,12) = -300(3.3744)(3.8960) = -\$3,943.97 \]
  7. Series 5 \u2014 \$350/yr withdrawals, t = 4 to 10 (7 payments). Value at t = 10, rolled forward 5 years: \[ -350(F/A,12\%,7)(F/P,12\%,5) = -350(10.0890)(1.7623) = -\$6,223.10 \]
  8. Series 6 \u2014 \$50 every 2 years, t = 2,4,\u2026,14 (7 payments). Since the spacing is 2 years, first convert the annual rate to an effective 2-year rate: \(i_2=(1.12)^2-1=25.440\%\). Then \((F/A,i_2,7)\) gives the value at t = 14 (i.e.\ 7 "2-year periods" after t = 0), and one more year at 12% carries it to t = 15: \[ -50(F/A,25.440\%,7)(1.12) = -50(15.2795)(1.12) = -\$855.65 \]
  9. Add all six series together. \[ F_{15} = 3,284.14 + 15,784.78 + 22,391.78 -3,943.97 -6,223.10 -855.65 \] \[ F_{15} = \$30,437.97 \]
Final Answer\(F_{15} \approx \$30,437.97\)
Q7

Reading a PW Equation Backwards into a Cash-Flow Diagram

PW = \u2212750 \u2212 100(P/A,10%,3)(P/F,10%,6) + 800(P/F,10%,8) \u2212 [200 \u2212 50(A/G,10%,5)](F/A,10%,5)(P/F,10%,14) + 600(F/A,10%,4)(P/F,10%,15). Draw the CFD.

Given
  • A pre-built PW equation with 4 distinct terms, i = 10% throughout
  • Task: decode each factor's timing rule and reconstruct the original cash flow diagram
Cash Flow Diagram
Step-by-Step Solution
  1. The key idea. Every TVM factor has a built-in "reference point" rule. To reverse-engineer a CFD from an equation, decode each term separately by asking: where does the factor's own reference point sit, and does an outer \((P/F,i,k)\) shift that reference point to actual time k?
  2. Term 1: \(-750\). A bare number with no factor attached is already a value at t = 0. So a \$750 outflow occurs at t = 0 (e.g., an initial investment/cost).
  3. Term 2: \(-100(P/A,10\%,3)(P/F,10\%,6)\). \((P/A,i,3)\) alone converts a 3-payment uniform series (occurring at "local" periods 1, 2, 3) into a present value at "local" period 0. The trailing \((P/F,10\%,6)\) then tells us that "local period 0" is actually actual t = 6. So the local periods 1, 2, 3 become actual t = 7, 8, 9: a \$100 outflow in each of those three years.
  4. Term 3: \(800(P/F,10\%,8)\). A single-payment factor directly says: an \$800 inflow occurs at t = 8 (adding to whatever is already happening that year from Term 2).
  5. Term 4: \(-[200-50(A/G,10\%,5)](F/A,10\%,5)(P/F,10\%,14)\). The bracket \([200-50(A/G,10\%,5)]\) is the equivalent uniform annual value of a decreasing arithmetic-gradient series: base \(A_1=200\), gradient \(G=50\), over 5 "local" periods (i.e., local cash flows of 200, 150, 100, 50, 0). Multiplying this equivalent A by \((F/A,10\%,5)\) moves it to the future value at "local period 5". The outer \((P/F,10\%,14)\) says that local period 5 is actual t = 14, so local period 1 is actual t = 10. The decoded outflows are: \$200 at t=10, \$150 at t=11, \$100 at t=12, \$50 at t=13, and \$0 at t=14 (so nothing is actually drawn at t = 14 for this term).
  6. Term 5: \(600(F/A,10\%,4)(P/F,10\%,15)\). \((F/A,i,4)\) gives the future value of a 4-payment uniform series at the time of its last payment (local period 4). The outer \((P/F,10\%,15)\) fixes local period 4 = actual t = 15, so the four \$600 inflows land at t = 12, 13, 14, 15.
  7. Assemble the full diagram (shown above): \$750 out at t=0; \$100 out at t=7,8,9; \$800 in at t=8; a decreasing \$200 \u2192 \$150 \u2192 \$100 \u2192 \$50 outflow at t=10,11,12,13; and \$600 in at t=12,13,14,15. Notice t=12,13 each carry two arrows (a gradient outflow and a uniform inflow) \u2014 both are drawn, since the diagram should mirror the equation term-for-term.
Final Answer\(The full cash-flow diagram is shown above \u2014 this question tests whether you can read TVM notation fluently in both directions (cash flow \(\to\) equation, and equation \(\to\) cash flow), which is exactly the skill exam MCQs on this topic probe.\)

Deferred Uniform Series — Present Worth 6 Years Before It Starts

With interest of 6%, what is the worth on December 31, 1994 of a series of end-of-year payments of \$317.70 made from years 2000 through 2004?

Given
  • Uniform payment, A = \$317.70/yr, for 2000–2004 (5 payments)
  • 1994 is t = 0; payments therefore fall at t = 6,7,8,9,10
  • i = 6% per year
  • Find: P at t = 0
Cash Flow Diagram
Step-by-Step Solution
  1. Recognize a deferred annuity. The 5 payments form an ordinary uniform series, but they don't start at t = 1 \u2014 the first payment is at t = 6. \((P/A,i,n)\) always values a series as of one period before its first payment. Here, "one period before t = 6" is t = 5 \u2014 not t = 0. So a second step (an extra \((P/F,i,5)\)) is needed to slide that value back to today.
  2. Write the working equation. \[ P = 317.70\,(P/A,6\%,5)\,(P/F,6\%,5) \]
  3. Compute the factors. \[ (P/A,6\%,5) = 4.2124, \qquad (P/F,6\%,5) = 0.7473 \]
  4. Substitute. \[ P = 317.70 \times 4.2124 \times 0.7473 = \$1,000.03 \]
Final Answer\(P \approx \$1,000.03\)

Ordinary Annuity vs. Annuity Due

You contribute \$3,000/year to a savings account at the end of each year for 10 years, earning 7%. How much can be withdrawn at the end of year 10? Now suppose the ten deposits are made at the beginning of each period — what is the balance at the end of period 10?

Given
  • A = \$3,000/yr, n = 10 years, i = 7%
  • Case 1: end-of-year deposits (ordinary annuity)
  • Case 2: beginning-of-year deposits (annuity due)
Cash Flow Diagram
Step-by-Step Solution
  1. Case 1 \u2014 ordinary annuity. Deposits at t = 1,\u2026,10; this is the textbook \((F/A,i,n)\) situation, which values a uniform series at the same time as the last payment. \[ F = 3000(F/A,7\%,10) \]
  2. \[ (F/A,7\%,10) = 13.8164 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F = 3000\times13.8164 = \$41,449.34 \]
  3. Case 2 \u2014 annuity due. Now each deposit happens one year earlier (t=0,\u2026,9), so each dollar earns one extra year of interest compared with Case 1. The whole ordinary-annuity result is simply multiplied by a single extra \((1+i)\): \[ F_{\text{due}} = 3000(F/A,7\%,10)(1+i) = F_{\text{ordinary}}\times(1.07) \]
  4. \[ F_{\text{due}} = 41,449.34\times1.07 = \$44,350.80 \]
Final Answer\(F_{\text{ordinary}} \approx \$41,449.34; \qquad F_{\text{due}} \approx \$44,350.80\)

Retirement Policy — Annuity-Due Premiums to a Lump-Sum Maturity

A 35-year-old pays an annual premium of Rs. 19,760, starting now, until age 49 (i.e., through age 49). At 8% compounded annually, what lump sum matures when the policy is claimed at age 50?

Given
  • Premium, A = Rs. 19,760/yr, paid at ages 35,36,…,49 (15 payments, first one paid ‘now’)
  • i = 8% per year
  • Maturity paid at age 50
  • Find: F at age 50
Cash Flow Diagram
Step-by-Step Solution
  1. Count the payments and place them on the timeline. Let "now" (age 35) be t = 0. Paying every year up to and including age 49 gives payments at t = 0,1,2,\u2026,14 \u2014 that's 15 payments, and because the first one is paid immediately, this is an annuity due. The maturity (age 50) falls at t = 15, which is exactly one period after the last premium \u2014 precisely where the standard \((F/A,i,n)\) reference point would sit if the series had instead started at t=1.
  2. Use the annuity-due future-value relation. For n payments starting at t=0, the value one period after the last payment is: \[ F = A(F/A,i,n)(1+i) \] (each of the n ordinary-annuity dollars simply earns one bonus year of interest because it was paid a year early).
  3. Compute. \[ (F/A,8\%,15) = 27.1521 \] \[ F = 19760 \times 27.1521 \times 1.08 = \text{Rs. }579,447.83 \]
Final Answer\(F \approx \text{Rs. }579,447.83\)
Q11

Comparing Three Investment Plans over 40 Years

Three plans for someone turning 24 today (i = 8%): (I) invest \$2,000/yr for the first 10 years, then let it grow untouched for 30 more years; (II) invest nothing for 10 years, then \$2,000/yr for the next 30 years; (III) invest \$2,000/yr for all 40 years. Deposits fall on birthdays, the first at n = 1 (25th birthday). Find the balance at n = 40 (64th birthday) for each.

Given
  • A = \$2,000/yr, i = 8%, n = 40 total years
  • Plan I: deposits n=1–10, then compounds alone to n=40
  • Plan II: deposits n=11–40
  • Plan III: deposits n=1–40
Cash Flow Diagram
Step-by-Step Solution
  1. Plan I. Find the value of the 10 deposits at n = 10 using \((F/A,i,10)\), then simply let that lump sum compound alone for the remaining 30 years using \((F/P,i,30)\) \u2014 no more deposits are added, so this is a pure single-payment step after n=10. \[ F_I = 2000(F/A,8\%,10)(F/P,8\%,30) \]
  2. \[ (F/A,8\%,10)=14.4866,\ \ (F/P,8\%,30)=10.0627 \] \[ F_I = 2000\times14.4866\times10.0627 = \$291,546.62 \]
  3. Plan II. Deposits run from n=11 to n=40, i.e., 30 consecutive deposits. Because \((F/A,i,30)\) automatically values a uniform series at the time of its own last payment (here, n = 40 \u2014 exactly where we want it), no extra shifting factor is needed: \[ F_{II} = 2000(F/A,8\%,30) \]
  4. \[ (F/A,8\%,30) = 113.2832 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F_{II} = 2000\times113.2832 = \$226,566.42 \]
  5. Plan III. A straightforward 40-payment uniform series ending exactly at n = 40: \[ F_{III} = 2000(F/A,8\%,40) \]
  6. \[ (F/A,8\%,40) = 259.0565 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F_{III} = 2000\times259.0565 = \$518,113.04 \]
  7. Compare and interpret. \(F_I=\$291,547 > F_{II}=\$226,566\), even though Plan I contributes only \$20,000 total (10 deposits) versus Plan II's \$60,000 (30 deposits)! This is the single most important intuition in engineering economics: money invested earliest, even in a smaller amount, benefits from far more compounding time. Plan III, which combines a long horizon with continuous contributions, dominates both, as expected.
Final Answer\(F_I \approx \$291,546.62, \quad F_{II} \approx \$226,566.42, \quad F_{III} \approx \$518,113.04\)

A recurring cost of \$C every k years, forever (first occurrence at year k), has present worth \(CC = \dfrac{C}{(1+i)^k - 1}\) \u2014 this comes from summing the infinite geometric series of \((P/F,i,k),(P/F,i,2k),\ldots\): \[ CC_{\text{rework}} = \dfrac{225000}{(1.08)^5 - 1} \]

  6. Total capitalized cost. \[ CC = CC_{\text{maint}} + CC_{\text{rework}} = 235,464.69 + 479,408.78 = \u20b9714,873.47 \]

Capitalized-Cost Comparison of Two Bridge Designs (Perpetual Service)

Type A bridge: \$75,000 initial cost, \$400/year maintenance forever. Type B bridge: \$40,000 to build, replaced every 30 years forever, \$700/year maintenance. At 6% interest, which alternative should be selected (capitalized cost approach)?

Given
  • Type A: first cost \$75,000, perpetual maintenance \$400/yr
  • Type B: first cost \$40,000 (recurring every 30 yrs, forever), perpetual maintenance \$700/yr
  • i = 6%/year
  • No salvage value for either
Cash Flow Diagram
Step-by-Step Solution
  1. Capitalized cost compares two "forever" alternatives on equal footing by finding the present worth of an infinite service life for each \u2014 the cheaper CC is the better economic choice (this is the standard method for public infrastructure with indefinite service lives, like bridges, dams, and parks).
  2. Type A \u2014 first cost plus a simple perpetuity of maintenance. \[ CC_A = 75000 + \dfrac{400}{i} = 75000 + \dfrac{400}{0.06} \] \[ CC_A = 75000 + 6,666.67 = \$81,666.67 \]
  3. Type B \u2014 two pieces: a recurring \$40,000 rebuild every 30 years, forever, plus a simple perpetuity of maintenance. For the recurring \$40,000 (first paid now, at t=0, then again at t=30, t=60, \u2026), the geometric-series capitalized-cost formula is: \[ CC_{\text{rebuild}} = \dfrac{40000}{1-(P/F,6\%,30)} \]
  4. \[ (P/F,6\%,30) = 0.1741 \quad\Rightarrow\quad CC_{\text{rebuild}} = \dfrac{40000}{1-0.1741} = \$48,432.61 \] \[ CC_B = 48,432.61 + \dfrac{700}{0.06} = 48,432.61 + 11,666.67 = \$60,099.27 \]
  5. Compare. \[ CC_A = \$81,666.67 \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad CC_B = \$60,099.27 \] Type B's capitalized cost is lower, even though it must be rebuilt periodically \u2014 the lower first cost and lower total maintenance outweigh the recurring-rebuild expense once everything is discounted at 6%.
Final Answer\(CC_A \approx \$81,666.67; \quad CC_B \approx \$60,099.27. \ \textbf{Select Type B}\text{ (lower capitalized cost).}\)
