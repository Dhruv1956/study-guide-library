Multivariate Normal Distribution & Hotelling's T²

Everything in univariate statistics you already know — means, variances, the normal curve, the t-test — has a "multivariate" version, built for when you're measuring several things about each individual at once instead of just one. This part builds that whole picture from scratch, ending at Hotelling's T², the multivariate cousin of the t-test.

1. What is multivariate data, and why do we need MVA?

Definition

Multivariate data is data involving more than one variable measured on each observation.

Worked example from your notes

Suppose you're analysing data on students. For each student you record: marks in Mathematics, marks in Science, marks in CS. That's 3 variables recorded on each student — this is multivariate data.

TypeMeaningExample
UnivariateOne variable per observationHeight of students
BivariateTwo variables per observationHeight & Weight
MultivariateMany variables per observationHeight, Weight, Age, Blood Pressure, etc.

A "variable" here really means a random variable — something that comes with its own mean, variance, and (when there's more than one) covariances and correlations with the other variables.

Other real-world examples of multivariate data

  • Weather data: temperature, wind speed, rainfall, air quality index.
  • Patient health records: blood pressure, cholesterol level, age, weight, heart rate.
  • Smartphone features: battery capacity, camera quality, screen size, RAM, price.
  • Employee performance: experience, number of projects completed, salary, work hours per week.
Why MVA is needed

Multivariate Analysis is the branch of statistics concerned with data that consists of measurements on several variables for each individual/object. It matters because most real-world problems are influenced by multiple factors at once — MVA lets us understand the interactions, patterns, and relationships between variables, not just look at each one in isolation.

The structure of multivariate data

Suppose we have measurements on \(p\) variables for each of \(n\) experimental units (people, products, etc). We write \(x_{ij}\) for the observed value of the \(j\)-th variable (\(j=1,\dots,p\)) on the \(i\)-th unit (\(i=1,\dots,n\)). This is gathered into an \((n \times p)\) data matrix — rows are observations, columns are variables:

\[ X = \begin{bmatrix} x_{11} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{1p} \\ x_{21} & x_{22} & \cdots & x_{2p} \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\ x_{n1} & x_{n2} & \cdots & x_{np} \end{bmatrix}_{n \times p} \]

2. Descriptive statistics for multivariate data

Descriptive statistics for MVD means summarising data involving more than one variable at a time. There are two central objects you build immediately: a mean vector and a covariance matrix.

The mean vector

Definition

The mean vector simply stacks the average of each variable into one column:

\[ X = \begin{bmatrix} X_1\\X_2\\ \vdots \\X_p \end{bmatrix} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \bar{X} = \begin{bmatrix} \bar{X}_1\\ \bar{X}_2\\ \vdots \\ \bar{X}_p \end{bmatrix} \]

The (sample) variance-covariance matrix

Definition

Captures the variability of each variable on its own (the diagonal) and the covariances (relationships) between every pair of variables (the off-diagonal). It's always symmetric — the relationship between \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) is the same as between \(X_2\) and \(X_1\).

\[ S = \begin{bmatrix} S_{11} & S_{12} & \cdots & S_{1p} \\ S_{21} & S_{22} & \cdots & S_{2p} \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\ S_{p1} & S_{p2} & \cdots & S_{pp} \end{bmatrix} \]

where, using the sample of size \(n\):

\[ S_{11} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum (X_1-\bar X_1)^2, \quad \dots, \quad S_{12}=S_{21}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum (X_1-\bar X_1)(X_2-\bar X_2) \]

Analogy

Think of the covariance matrix as a "relationship report card" for your variables. The diagonal entries are each variable's own "spread" (variance) — how much it wobbles around its own average. The off-diagonal entries tell you whether two variables tend to move together (positive covariance), move opposite to each other (negative), or don't influence each other linearly at all (near zero).

Your notes also mention the correlation matrix \(R\) — same idea, but every entry is standardised to lie in \([-1,1]\), so you can compare relationship strengths across variables measured in totally different units.

3. Multivariate distributions, random vectors & expectation

Definition

A multivariate distribution describes the underlying random structure of an entire vector of random variables at once — not just one variable. From it we can derive the marginal properties of each individual variable, and it also describes relationships between variables or groups of variables.

A random vector is simply a vector whose entries are themselves random variables. Similarly, a random matrix is a matrix whose entries are random variables. The expected value of a random vector/matrix is defined entry-by-entry:

\[ X = \begin{bmatrix}X_1\\X_2\\\vdots\\X_p\end{bmatrix} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad E(X) = \begin{bmatrix}E(X_1)\\E(X_2)\\\vdots\\E(X_p)\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}\mu_1\\\mu_2\\\vdots\\\mu_p\end{bmatrix} = \mu \]

This \(\mu\) is the population mean vector — the true underlying average, as opposed to \(\bar X\) which is the average we actually compute from a sample.

4. The population covariance matrix and correlation coefficient

Definition — Covariance matrix (a.k.a. Dispersion matrix)

\[ \Sigma = E\big[(X-\mu)(X-\mu)^{\top}\big] = \begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{11} & \sigma_{12} & \cdots \\ \sigma_{12} & \sigma_{22} & \cdots \\ \vdots & & \ddots \end{bmatrix} \]

Let's expand this for \(p=2\) variables to see exactly where each entry comes from:

\[ \Sigma = E\begin{bmatrix} (X_1-\mu_1)^2 & (X_1-\mu_1)(X_2-\mu_2) \\ (X_2-\mu_2)(X_1-\mu_1) & (X_2-\mu_2)^2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{11} & \sigma_{12}\\ \sigma_{21} & \sigma_{22}\end{bmatrix} \]

Here \(\sigma_{11}=\text{Var}(X_1)\), \(\sigma_{22}=\text{Var}(X_2)\), and \(\sigma_{12}=\sigma_{21}=\text{Cov}(X_1,X_2)\).

Worked example

If \(\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix}9 & 3\\3 & 4\end{bmatrix}\), then \(V(X_1)=9 \Rightarrow \text{S.D}(X_1)=3\), and \(V(X_2)=4 \Rightarrow \text{S.D}(X_2)=2\).

Definition — Population correlation coefficient

\[ \rho_{ik} = \frac{\sigma_{ik}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{ii}}\sqrt{\sigma_{kk}}} = \frac{\text{Covariance}}{\text{S.D}(X_i)\cdot \text{S.D}(X_k)} \]

It measures the amount of linear association between the random variables \(X_i\) and \(X_k\).

5. The Multivariate Normal Distribution (MND)

Your notes build this up exactly the right way: start from the familiar one-variable (univariate) normal curve, then ask what changes when you have several correlated variables at once.

Recall: the normal distribution in one dimension

\[ X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2), \qquad f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \, e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^2}, \qquad -\infty < x < \infty \]

The motivating question from your notes

But what if we want to model multiple continuous variables together — say height, weight, and age of students — which may be correlated? We need a distribution that can capture this multivariate behaviour, especially the relationships (covariances) between the variables.

Definition

A random vector \(X=(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_p)'\) follows a Multivariate Normal Distribution (MND) with mean vector \(\mu\) and covariance matrix \(\Sigma\), written \(X \sim N_p(\mu,\Sigma)\).

Building the density function — the key insight

Look at the exponent of the univariate normal density: \(\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^2\). Your notes point out this can be rewritten as \((x-\mu)(\sigma^2)^{-1}(x-\mu)\) — i.e. "deviation, times inverse-variance, times deviation." That's exactly the form that generalises cleanly to \(p\) dimensions: just replace the scalar deviation with a vector deviation, and the scalar \(\sigma^2\) with the matrix \(\Sigma\):

\[ (X-\mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(X-\mu) \quad \longrightarrow \quad \textbf{Mahalanobis distance} \]

Analogy — what the Mahalanobis distance is really doing

Ordinary (Euclidean) distance from the mean would treat every direction in the data equally. But real variables have different spreads and are correlated with each other. The Mahalanobis distance corrects for this: it measures "how many standard-deviations away" a point is, after accounting for the shape and orientation of the data's spread. A point that's far away along a direction where the data barely varies counts as "more unusual" than the same physical distance in a direction where the data naturally spreads out a lot.

Definition — the MND density function

For a random vector \(X=(X_1,\dots,X_p)'\):

\[ f(x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{p/2}\,|\Sigma|^{1/2}} \exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\right], \qquad -\infty < x_i < \infty,\ i=1,\dots,p \]

Notation: \(X \sim N_p(\mu,\Sigma)\) — directly analogous to the notation for the normal density in the univariate case.

6. Properties of the MND

The following are true for a random vector \(X\) having an MND:

  1. Linear combinations of the components of \(X\) are normally distributed.
  2. All subsets of the components of \(X\) also have an MND.
  3. Zero covariance implies the corresponding components are independent.
  4. The conditional distributions of the components are multivariate normal.

Result 1 — a single linear combination

Result

If \(X\) is distributed as \(N_p(\mu,\Sigma)\), then any linear combination \(a'X = a_1X_1+a_2X_2+\dots+a_pX_p\) is distributed as \(N(a'\mu,\; a'\Sigma a)\).

Worked example — sanity check

Take \(a'=[1,0,\dots,0]\), so \(a'X = X_1\). Then \(a'\mu=\mu_1\), and \(a'\Sigma a = \sigma_{11}\) (you can verify this by directly multiplying out the matrices — the "1,0,...,0" simply picks out the top-left entry of \(\Sigma\)). So the result correctly tells us \(X_1 \sim N(\mu_1,\sigma_{11})\) — exactly what we'd expect, since \(X_1\) alone is just one of the components.

Result 2 — several linear combinations at once

Result

For a fixed \(q\times p\) matrix \(A\), the vector \(AX\) is distributed as \(N_q(A\mu,\; A\Sigma A')\).

Worked example

Let \(X \sim N_3(\mu,\Sigma)\). Find the distribution of \(\begin{bmatrix}X_1-X_2\\X_2-X_3\end{bmatrix}\).

Here \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1&0\\0&1&-1\end{bmatrix}\). Then:

\[ A\mu = \begin{bmatrix}\mu_1-\mu_2\\\mu_2-\mu_3\end{bmatrix} \]

\[ A\Sigma A' = \begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{11}-2\sigma_{12}+\sigma_{22} & \sigma_{12}+\sigma_{23}-\sigma_{22}-\sigma_{13}\\ \sigma_{12}+\sigma_{23}-\sigma_{22}-\sigma_{13} & \sigma_{22}-2\sigma_{23}+\sigma_{33}\end{bmatrix} \]

This is exactly the kind of "variance of a difference" formula you'd expect from univariate stats — \(\text{Var}(X_1-X_2)=\sigma_{11}-2\sigma_{12}+\sigma_{22}\) — just extended to a 2-dimensional output.

Result 3 — every subset of variables is itself MND

Partition \(X\), its mean \(\mu\), and its covariance \(\Sigma\) into blocks:

\[ X=\begin{bmatrix}X_1\\X_2\end{bmatrix},\quad \mu=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_1\\\mu_2\end{bmatrix},\quad \Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{11}&\Sigma_{12}\\\Sigma_{21}&\Sigma_{22}\end{bmatrix} \]

Then \(X_1\) alone is distributed as \(N_q(\mu_1,\Sigma_{11})\) — you can simply "crop" the mean vector and the top-left block of the covariance matrix.

Worked example

\(X \sim N_5(\mu,\Sigma)\). Find the distribution of \(\begin{bmatrix}X_2\\X_4\end{bmatrix}\). Simply pick out rows/columns 2 and 4 of \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix}X_2\\X_4\end{bmatrix} \sim N_2\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mu_2\\\mu_4\end{bmatrix},\; \begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{22}&\sigma_{24}\\\sigma_{42}&\sigma_{44}\end{bmatrix}\right) \]

Result 4 — independence and zero covariance

(a)If \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) are independent, then \(\text{Cov}(X_1,X_2)=0\) (a matrix of zeros).
(b)If \(\begin{bmatrix}X_1\\X_2\end{bmatrix} \sim N_{q_1+q_2}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mu_1\\\mu_2\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{11}&\Sigma_{12}\\\Sigma_{21}&\Sigma_{22}\end{bmatrix}\right)\), then \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) are independent if and only if \(\Sigma_{12}=0\).
(c)Conversely, if \(X_1 \sim N_{q_1}(\mu_1,\Sigma_{11})\) and \(X_2 \sim N_{q_2}(\mu_2,\Sigma_{22})\) are independent, then jointly \(\begin{bmatrix}X_1\\X_2\end{bmatrix}\) has an MND with a block-diagonal covariance matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{11}&0\\0&\Sigma_{22}\end{bmatrix}\).
Key takeaway — this is special to the normal distribution!

In general statistics, "zero covariance" does not imply independence — two variables can have zero linear relationship while still being tightly dependent in a non-linear way. Result (b) above is a special, powerful fact that's only true because we're inside the MND world: for jointly normal variables, zero covariance is enough to guarantee full independence.

Worked example

\(X \sim N_3(\mu,\Sigma)\) with \(\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix}4&1&0\\1&3&0\\0&0&2\end{bmatrix}\). Are \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) independent? What about \((X_1,X_2)\) and \(X_3\)?

\(\text{Cov}(X_1,X_2)=\sigma_{12}=1 \neq 0\) → not independent. But the covariance between \((X_1,X_2)\) and \(X_3\) is the block \(\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}\) → independent. So \(X_3\) is independent of \(X_1\) and also of \(X_2\), even though \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) are not independent of each other.

7. Worked numerical problems on the properties

Problem 1 — mean and covariance of a linear combination

Worked example

Given \(\mu=\begin{bmatrix}4\\-1\\5\end{bmatrix}\), \(\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&0.5\\1&3&1\\0.5&1&2.5\end{bmatrix}\), and \(Y=2X_1+3X_2-X_3\). Find the mean and variance of \(Y\).

Here \(a'=[2,3,-1]\). Mean: \(a'\mu = 2(4)+3(-1)-1(5) = 8-3-5 = \mathbf{0}\).

Variance: \(a'\Sigma a\). First compute \(\Sigma a = [2(2)+1(3)+0.5(-1),\; 1(2)+3(3)+1(-1),\; 0.5(2)+1(3)+2.5(-1)] = [6.5,\ 10,\ 1.5]\). Then \(a'\Sigma a = 2(6.5)+3(10)+(-1)(1.5) = 13+30-1.5 = \mathbf{41.5}\).

This matches your notes exactly (\(a'\Sigma a = 41.5\)) — confirmed correct.

Problem 2 — mean vector of two linear combinations

Worked example

\(X \sim N_3(\mu,\Sigma)\), \(\mu=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{bmatrix}\). Define \(Y_1=2X_1+X_2-X_3\), \(Y_2=-X_1+3X_2+2X_3\).

Here \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&-1\\-1&3&2\end{bmatrix}\). Mean of \(Y\): \(A\mu = \begin{bmatrix}2(1)+1(2)-1(3)\\-1(1)+3(2)+2(3)\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\11\end{bmatrix}\).

Matches your notes' answer of \([1,\,11]'\) — confirmed correct.

Common confusion — checking independence of a linear combination

What about something like \(\left(\frac{X_1+X_2}{2}\right)\) and \(X_3\)? You can't just "read off" a block here, because \(\frac{X_1+X_2}{2}\) isn't one of the original variables — it's a linear combination. Worked example (this exact case, independently re-verified)

With \(\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}1&-2&0\\-2&5&0\\0&0&2\end{bmatrix}\), \(A=\begin{bmatrix}\frac12&\frac12&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\), computing \(A\Sigma A'\) gives \(\begin{bmatrix}\frac12 & 0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}\) — the off-diagonal is 0, so \(\frac{X_1+X_2}{2}\) and \(X_3\) are independent.

We want the distribution of Marks (X) given Study Hours (Y). If \(Y=2\) hours, Marks \(\in\{50,55,60\}\), each with probability \(\frac13\) — this is the conditional distribution of X given \(Y=2\). Similarly, if \(Y=5\) hours, Marks \(\in\{70,75,80\}\), each with probability \(\frac13\). The conditional distribution tells us how marks are distributed once we already know how many hours a student studied — we've restricted our focus to a smaller, more specific slice of the data.

Definition — the general formula

Let \(X=\begin{bmatrix}X_1\\X_2\end{bmatrix} \sim N_p(\mu,\Sigma)\) with \(\mu=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_1\\\mu_2\end{bmatrix}\), \(\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{11}&\Sigma_{12}\\\Sigma_{21}&\Sigma_{22}\end{bmatrix}\). Then the conditional distribution of \(X_1\) given \(X_2=x_2\) is normal, with:

\[ \text{Mean} = \mu_1 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(x_2-\mu_2) \]

\[ \text{Variance} = \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} \]

Notice the variance formula does not depend on the particular value \(x_2\) — only the mean shifts depending on what you condition on.

Common confusion — can the conditional mean/variance be negative?

The conditional mean can absolutely come out negative — it's just a shifted average, and can take any real value depending on the data (imagine the average bank balance in a city being negative on average, due to debts). But the conditional variance can never be negative — it's an expected squared deviation, and a square is always \(\geq 0\).

Worked Example 1

Worked example

9. Hotelling's T² Statistic (one sample)

This is the destination this whole part has been building toward: the multivariate version of the humble t-test.

Starting from the univariate case

In univariate statistics, to test a hypothesis about a population mean \(\mu\) against a specific value \(\mu_0\):

\[ H_0: \mu=\mu_0 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_1:\mu\neq\mu_0 \]

For small samples (say \(n\leq30\)) we use the t-distribution: \(t=\dfrac{\bar x-\mu}{s/\sqrt n}\), where \(\bar x\) is the sample mean, \(\mu\) the population mean, \(s\) the sample standard deviation.

Now translate every symbol to its multivariate counterpart: \(\bar x \to \bar X\), \(\mu \to \mu\), \(s^2 \to S\) (the sample covariance matrix), \(n\to n\). Then:

\[ t = \sqrt n\,(\bar x-\mu)\,s^{-1} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad t^2 = n(\bar x-\mu)^2(s^2)^{-1} \]

Generalising \((\bar x-\mu)^2\) to a vector version — \((\bar X-\mu)'\) times \((\bar X-\mu)\) — gives exactly:

Definition — Hotelling's T² statistic (one sample)

\[ T^2 = n(\bar X-\mu)^{\top} S^{-1}(\bar X-\mu) \]

where \(\bar X \sim N_p(\mu,\Sigma/n)\) and \(S \sim W_p(n-1,\Sigma)\) (the Wishart distribution — covered in Part 3). Under \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\):

\[ T^2 = n(\bar X-\mu_0)^{\top}S^{-1}(\bar X-\mu_0) \sim \frac{(n-1)p}{n-p}\,F_{p,\,n-p} \qquad (\text{for } n-p<30) \]

Analogy

Just like the univariate \(t^2\) statistic measures "how many standard errors away is my sample mean from the hypothesised value, squared," Hotelling's \(T^2\) measures the same thing in \(p\) dimensions at once — using the Mahalanobis distance (from Section 5!) between \(\bar X\) and \(\mu_0\), scaled by the sample size. It's really the same idea as the t-test, just wearing multivariate clothing.

Worked Example 1 — setting up the formula symbolically

Worked example

A random sample of \(n=20\) was collected from a bivariate normal process, with \(\bar X=\begin{bmatrix}10\\20\end{bmatrix}\), \(S=\begin{bmatrix}40&-50\\-50&100\end{bmatrix}\). Find \(T^2\) (as a function of \(\mu_1,\mu_2\)) and its distribution.

\(|S|=40(100)-(-50)(-50)=1500\). \(S^{-1}=\frac{1}{1500}\begin{bmatrix}100&50\\50&40\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{15}&\frac{1}{30}\\\frac{1}{30}&\frac{2}{75}\end{bmatrix}\).

Multiplying out \(T^2=20(\bar X-\mu)'S^{-1}(\bar X-\mu)\) gives:

\[ T^2 = 1.33(10-\mu_1)^2 + 1.33(10-\mu_1)(20-\mu_2) + 0.53(20-\mu_2)^2 \]

Distribution: \(T^2 \sim \dfrac{(n-1)p}{n-p}F_{p,n-p} = \dfrac{19\times2}{18}F_{2,18} = \dfrac{19}{9}F_{2,18}\). Using the tabulated critical value \(F_{2,18}(0.05)=3.56\), the critical value of \(T^2\) is \(\dfrac{19}{9}\times3.56 = \mathbf{7.51}\).

10. Hotelling's T² Statistic (two samples)

What if instead of comparing one sample's mean to a fixed hypothesised value, you want to compare the means of two independent samples — e.g. "do these two machines produce the same average output"?

Definition

\[ H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_1:\mu_1\neq\mu_2 \]

First combine (pool) the two samples' covariance information:

\[ S_{\text{pooled}} = \frac{(n_1-1)S_1+(n_2-1)S_2}{n_1+n_2-2} \]

Then the two-sample Hotelling's \(T^2\) statistic is:

\[ T^2 = \frac{n_1n_2}{n_1+n_2}(\bar X_1-\bar X_2)^{\top}S_p^{-1}(\bar X_1-\bar X_2) \sim \frac{(n_1+n_2-2)p}{n_1+n_2-p-1}\,F_{p,\;n_1+n_2-p-1} \]

Use the F-distribution when \(n_1+n_2-p-1 < 30\); for large samples (\(\geq30\)) it can be approximated by a Chi-square distribution instead (see Section 11).

Worked Example

Worked example

Two manufacturing machines (A and B) are compared on two quality characteristics \(X_1,X_2\), each with sample size \(n_1=n_2=4\):

\[ T^2 = \frac{4\times4}{4+4}\,[2.5,\,2.75]\,S_p^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}2.5\\2.75\end{bmatrix} = 2\times12.97 = \mathbf{25.94} \]

11. Large samples: when T² becomes a Chi-square

Key rule

When the relevant degrees of freedom are small (\(n_1+n_2-p-1<30\)), use the exact \(F\)-distribution as above. When they're large (\(n_1+n_2-p-1\geq30\)), \(T^2\) can be approximated by a Chi-square distribution with \(p\) degrees of freedom: \(T^2\sim\chi^2_p\).

Worked example

Fifty bars of soap are manufactured in each of two ways. Two characteristics, \(X_1=\)lather, \(X_2=\)mildness, are measured. Survey statistics: \(\bar X_1=\begin{bmatrix}8.3\\4.1\end{bmatrix}\), \(S_1=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&6\end{bmatrix}\), \(\bar X_2=\begin{bmatrix}10.2\\3.9\end{bmatrix}\), \(S_2=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&4\end{bmatrix}\). Test \(H_0:\mu_1-\mu_2=0\) at 5% LOS.

\(n_1=n_2=50\), so \(S_p=\dfrac{S_1+S_2}{2}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&5\end{bmatrix}\). \(|S_p|=10-1=9\), so \(S_p^{-1}=\dfrac19\begin{bmatrix}5&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\).

\(\bar X_1-\bar X_2 = \begin{bmatrix}-1.9\\0.2\end{bmatrix}\). With \(\frac{n_1n_2}{n_1+n_2}=\frac{2500}{100}=25\):

\[ T^2_{cal} = 25\times2.0973 = \mathbf{52.43} \]

Check degrees of freedom: \(n_1+n_2-p-1 = 50+50-2-1=97 \geq 30\) → use \(\chi^2_p\) instead of \(F\). Critical value: \(\chi^2_2(0.05)=5.99\).

Since \(T^2_{cal}=52.43 \gg \chi^2_2(0.05)=5.99\), we reject \(H_0\) at 5% — strong evidence the two production methods give different mean characteristics. (This conclusion in your notes is correct.)

Key takeaway for all of Part 1

Everything here mirrors univariate statistics, just "thickened" from scalars into vectors and matrices: mean → mean vector, variance → covariance matrix, \((x-\mu)^2/\sigma^2\) → Mahalanobis distance \((x-\mu)'\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\), and the t-test → Hotelling's \(T^2\) test. The MND's special properties (linear combinations stay normal, zero covariance ⟹ independence, conditionals stay normal) are what make all of this tractable.

Multiple & Partial Correlation

Ordinary correlation only ever looks at two variables at a time — and can be seriously misleading when a third variable is secretly influencing both. This part builds the two tools multivariate analysis uses to fix that.

1. What simple correlation can't tell you

Until now, how many variables were involved in the correlation coefficient? Just two: \(r_{12}=\text{Corr}(X_1,X_2)\).

The question your notes pose

Does this correlation consider the effect of other variables? No. It measures only the linear association between two variables, full stop.

Worked example — Height and Weight

Suppose \(r=0.82\) between Height and Weight. Can we conclude Height alone explains Weight? Not necessarily — this is exactly why multivariate analysis introduces multiple and partial correlation.

Motivating with three variables

Let \(X_1=\)Weight, \(X_2=\)Height, \(X_3=\)Age. Does age also affect weight? If age influences both height and weight, is the simple correlation between height and weight telling the whole story? No — this motivates partial correlation.

2. Partial correlation

Definition

Partial correlation measures the relationship between two variables after removing the effect of one or more other variables. We first remove the effect of age from both height and weight, then correlate what's left over ("the remaining parts").

\[ r_{12.3} = \text{Correlation between } X_1 \text{ and } X_2, \text{ given } X_3 \]

Worked example

Suppose we're studying Salary, Experience, and Education. The simple correlation of Salary vs. Experience might look strong — but Education affects both. Partial correlation asks: what is the relationship between Salary and Experience, after controlling for Education?

Formula

\[ r_{12.3} = \frac{r_{12} - r_{13}r_{23}}{\sqrt{(1-r_{13}^2)(1-r_{23}^2)}} \]

We're adjusting the original correlation by removing the influence of \(X_3\) from both the \(X_1\)-\(X_2\) relationship.

Formal definition via the covariance matrix

Suppose \(X\) is a random vector with covariance matrix \(\Sigma\). Split \(X\) into \(\tilde X_{(1)}=X_1\) (one variable) and \(\tilde X_{(2)}=(X_2,\dots,X_p)'\) (the rest):

\[ \Sigma_{p\times p} = \begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{11}&\Sigma_{12}\\\Sigma_{21}&\Sigma_{22}\end{bmatrix} \]

The partial correlation between \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) while controlling for other variables \((X_3,\dots,X_p)\) measures the direct association between \(X_1\) and \(X_2\), after removing the influence of the other variables.

3. Multiple correlation

The motivating question

Suppose we want to predict Weight using Height and Age. Should we correlate weight separately with height, then separately with age? No — we want both variables considered together.

Definition

Multiple correlation \(R_{1.23}\) measures how well a group of variables \((X_2,X_3)\) jointly predicts another variable \(X_1\).

\[ R_{1.23} = \sqrt{\frac{r_{12}^2+r_{13}^2-2r_{12}r_{13}r_{23}}{1-r_{23}^2}} \]

Formal definition via the covariance matrix

The multiple correlation coefficient measures how well one dependent variable can be predicted using a set of independent variables. With \(X=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde X_{(1)}\\\tilde X_{(2)}\end{bmatrix}\), \(\tilde X_{(1)}=X_1\) (dependent), \(\tilde X_{(2)}=(X_2,\dots,X_p)'\) (independent), the multiple correlation coefficient is denoted \(R_{1.23\dots p}\).

Key takeaway — what R² actually measures

\(R_{1.23}=\text{Corr}(X_1,\hat X_1)\) where \(\hat X_1\) is \(X_1\)'s best linear prediction from the other variables. It measures how strongly \(X_1\) is associated with its best linear prediction from the other variables:

\[ R_{1.23}^2 = \frac{\text{Explained variance}}{\text{Total variance}}, \qquad R_{1.23}=\sqrt{R_{1.23}^2}, \qquad 0\leq R_{1.23}\leq1 \]

It measures the strength of the joint linear relationship, not its direction.

Why can partial correlation be negative?

It's still a correlation between two variables (after removing the effect of others) — so it keeps every property of an ordinary correlation, including the ability to be negative. For example, \(r_{12.3}=\text{Cor}(X_1,X_2\mid X_3)\) can be \(-0.7\), \(0\), or \(+0.7\).

Analogy — why

Because after controlling for \(X_3\), the remaining parts of \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) can still move in either direction relative to each other. For example, after removing the effect of age: as the remaining part of Height rises, the remaining part of Weight might decrease — so the partial correlation is negative.

Why can multiple correlation never be negative?

\(R_{1.23}=\text{Corr}(X_1,\hat X_1)\) where \(\hat X_1\) is the best linear prediction of \(X_1\) built from \(X_2\) and \(X_3\) together. Since it's specifically defined as the positive square root \(R_{1.23}=\sqrt{R_{1.23}^2}\), it measures the strength of the joint relationship, never its direction — so it can never be negative.

Wishart Distribution & Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Part 1 used the sample covariance matrix \(S\) as if we already knew its distribution — this part derives exactly that (the Wishart distribution), and then derives \(S\) itself from first principles, by finding the maximum likelihood estimators of \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\).

Page 1

1. From Chi-square to Wishart

The motivating question

In univariate statistics, what distribution do we get when we add up squared standard normal observations?

Suppose \(Z_1,Z_2,\dots,Z_n \sim N(0,1)\) independently. Then:

\[ Z_1^2+Z_2^2+\dots+Z_n^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n Z_i^2 \sim \chi_n^2 \]

The key transition

What if, instead of one variable, we have several normally distributed variables? Suppose \(X_i=\begin{bmatrix}X_{i1}\\X_{i2}\\\vdots\\X_{ip}\end{bmatrix}\) is a \(p\)-dimensional random vector, with \(X_i \sim N_p(0,\Sigma)\).

Now, instead of calculating one quantity like \(\sum Z_i^2\), we need to calculate something involving the squares and cross-products of several variables at once. This is exactly the transition from "one variable, squared and summed" to "several variables, whose squares and cross-products all get summed together into a matrix."

Page 2

2. The cross-product matrix

With two variables, instead of a single sum of squares, we'd want \(\sum X_i^2\), \(\sum Y_i^2\), and \(\sum X_iY_i\) — all three at once. This motivates writing the whole collection as a matrix.

Definition

For observations \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\) (each a \(p\)-dimensional vector), define the cross-product matrix:

\[ A = \sum_{i=1}^n X_iX_i^{\top} \]

This is a \(p\times p\) matrix.

Worked example — expanding for p=2

For \(p=2\), write \(X_i=\begin{bmatrix}X_i\\Y_i\end{bmatrix}\). Then \(X_iX_i^\top=\begin{bmatrix}X_i^2&X_iY_i\\X_iY_i&Y_i^2\end{bmatrix}\), so:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix}\sum X_i^2 & \sum X_iY_i\\ \sum X_iY_i & \sum Y_i^2\end{bmatrix} \]

This matrix contains all the sums of squares and cross-products at once. In multivariate analysis, this matrix plays a role directly analogous to the "sum of squares" you're used to from univariate statistics.

Now, introducing the Wishart distribution

Just as the Chi-square distribution describes the distribution of the sum of squared normal variables, the Wishart distribution describes the distribution of this matrix \(A\) of sums of squares and cross-products, when the observations come from a multivariate normal distribution.

Page 3

3. Defining the Wishart distribution

Definition

If \(X_i \sim N_p(0,\Sigma)\) independently (\(i=1,\dots,n\)), then:

\[ A = \sum_{i=1}^n X_iX_i^{\top} \sim W_p(n,\Sigma) \]

where: \(p=\)dimension of the random vector, \(n=\)degrees of freedom, \(\Sigma=\)covariance matrix, and \(A\) is a random \(p\times p\) symmetric matrix.

For \(p=1\), the Wishart distribution reduces exactly to the Chi-square distribution. "The Wishart distribution is a multivariate generalisation of the Chi-square distribution."

Why do we need the Wishart distribution?

Suppose \(X_1,\dots,X_n \sim N_p(\mu,\Sigma)\). Then the sample covariance matrix is:

\[ S = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar X)(X_i-\bar X)^{\top} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad (n-1)S \sim W_p(n-1,\Sigma) \]

Analogy — direct parallel with the univariate case

In univariate statistics you already know \(\dfrac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}\) (we lose one degree of freedom because we had to estimate the mean first, before computing the sample variance). Exactly the same story here: \((n-1)S \sim W_p(n-1,\Sigma)\) — we use \(n-1\), not \(n\), because we spent one degree of freedom estimating the mean vector \(\mu\).

This is precisely the fact that Part 1's Hotelling's \(T^2\) formula relied on: \(S \sim W_p(n-1,\Sigma)\) is exactly what makes the \(T^2\) statistic's sampling distribution (the \(F\)-distribution) derivable.

4. Three key properties of the Wishart distribution

Your notes develop three "basic operations" you can perform on a Wishart-distributed matrix: adding two of them, transforming one by a fixed matrix, and squeezing one down into a quadratic form. Each one directly mirrors a familiar Chi-square fact.

Property 1 — Adding independent Wishart matrices

Result

If \(A_1 \sim W_{n_1}(\Sigma)\) independently of \(A_2 \sim W_{n_2}(\Sigma)\), then \(A_1+A_2 \sim W_{n_1+n_2}(\Sigma)\). In words: degrees of freedom simply add.

Analogy

This is the exact matrix version of a fact you already know: if \(X\sim\chi^2_{n_1}\) and \(Y\sim\chi^2_{n_2}\) independently, then \(X+Y\sim\chi^2_{n_1+n_2}\). "Wishart's additivity property is the matrix version of the Chi-square addition property."

▸ Show the proof

Write \(A=\sum_{i=1}^n X_iX_i'\) where \(X_i\sim N_p(0,\Sigma)\) independently. If \(A_1\) uses \(n_1\) such vectors and \(A_2\) (independently) uses \(n_2\) more, then \(A_1+A_2\) is simply the sum-of-cross-products over all \(n_1+n_2\) vectors combined — which by definition is Wishart with \(n_1+n_2\) degrees of freedom.

Property 2 — Transforming a Wishart matrix

Result

If \(A \sim W_p(n,\Sigma)\) and \(C\) is a fixed \(q\times p\) matrix, then \(CAC' \sim W_q(n,\,C\Sigma C')\).

▸ Show the proof

Start from \(A=\sum_i X_iX_i'\). Then:

\[ CAC' = C\left(\sum_i X_iX_i'\right)C' = \sum_i (CX_i)(CX_i)' \]

Since \(X_i\sim N_p(0,\Sigma)\), a linear transformation gives \(CX_i \sim N_q(0,\,C\Sigma C')\). So \(CAC'=\sum_i(CX_i)(CX_i)'\) is exactly a sum-of-cross-products of \(N_q(0,C\Sigma C')\)-distributed vectors — which by definition is \(W_q(n,C\Sigma C')\). \(\blacksquare\)

Worked example — what C is actually doing

Suppose \(X=(X_1,X_2,X_3)'\) (so \(p=3\)). If we choose \(C=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{bmatrix}\), then \(CX=\begin{bmatrix}X_1\\X_2\end{bmatrix}\) — i.e. \(C\) simply selects a subset of the original variables. Here the original vector has dimension \(p=3\), but after the transformation we have dimension \(q=2\). So the Wishart changes from \(W_3\) to \(W_2\) — this is exactly the matrix version of "any subset of an MND vector is itself MND," from Part 1!

Property 3 — Quadratic form: Wishart collapses back to Chi-square

Result

If \(A\sim W_p(n,\Sigma)\) and \(a\) is a fixed \(p\times1\) vector, then:

\[ a'Aa \sim (a'\Sigma a)\,\chi^2_n \qquad \text{or equivalently} \qquad \frac{a'Aa}{a'\Sigma a} \sim \chi^2_n \]

This is the Wishart distribution folding all the way back down to a plain Chi-square — a nice sanity check that the whole framework is self-consistent.

▸ Show the proof

Start with \(A=\sum_i X_iX_i'\). Then \(a'Aa = a'\left(\sum_iX_iX_i'\right)a = \sum_{i=1}^n a'X_iX_i'a = \sum_{i=1}^n(a'X_i)^2\) (since \(a'X_iX_i'a=(a'X_i)(X_i'a)=(a'X_i)^2\), as \(a'X_i\) is a scalar).

Now, since \(X_i\sim N_p(0,\Sigma)\), the linear combination \(a'X_i\) is normally distributed with \(E(a'X_i)=0\) and \(V(a'X_i)=a'\Sigma a\), i.e. \(a'X_i \sim N(0,a'\Sigma a)\).

Standardise: \(Z_i = \dfrac{a'X_i}{\sqrt{a'\Sigma a}} \sim N(0,1)\). Therefore:

\[ \frac{a'Aa}{a'\Sigma a} = \sum_{i=1}^n\left(\frac{a'X_i}{\sqrt{a'\Sigma a}}\right)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n Z_i^2 \]

But we already know \(\sum_i Z_i^2 \sim \chi^2_n\) (from Section 1!). So \(\dfrac{a'Aa}{a'\Sigma a}\sim\chi^2_n\). \(\blacksquare\)

5. Sampling from an MND & setting up Maximum Likelihood

Suppose we take a random sample from a multivariate normal population. What information do we want to estimate?

\[ X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n \overset{iid}{\sim} N_p(\mu,\Sigma) \]

where \(\mu=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_1\\\mu_2\\\vdots\\\mu_p\end{bmatrix}\) is the population mean vector and \(\Sigma\) is the population covariance matrix. Since \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\) are both unknown, how can we estimate them from the sample? This leads directly to Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE).

The sample, laid out as a matrix

For \(p=2\), each observation is \(X_i=\begin{bmatrix}X_{i1}\\X_{i2}\end{bmatrix}\), and the full sample looks like a data matrix with each column being one variable and each row being one individual — exactly the same layout as Part 1, Section 1.

The Maximum Likelihood idea, in one sentence

"We choose the parameter values that make the observed sample most likely."

For a single observation, the MND density (from Part 1) gives:

\[ f(x\mid \mu,\Sigma) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{p/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}} \exp\left[-\frac12(x_i-\mu)'\Sigma^{-1}(x_i-\mu)\right] \]

Since the observations are independent, the full likelihood function is their product:

\[ L(\mu,\Sigma) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i,\mu,\Sigma) \]

We now need to find the values of \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\) that maximise this likelihood. Taking logs (which turns the product into a sum, and is easier to differentiate — and doesn't change where the maximum occurs, since log is increasing):

\[ \ell = \log L(\mu,\Sigma) = -\frac{np}{2}\log(2\pi) - \frac{n}{2}\log|\Sigma| - \frac12\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\mu)'\Sigma^{-1}(x_i-\mu) \]

Now we differentiate this with respect to \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\) separately, and set each derivative to zero.

6. MLE of the mean vector

We want \(\dfrac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu}=0\). The only \(\mu\)-dependent part of \(\ell\) is the quadratic-form sum, so let's expand that term carefully.

Step 1 — expand the quadratic form

Consider \(Q_i=(x_i-\mu)'B(x_i-\mu)\) where we write \(B=\Sigma^{-1}\) for brevity. Expanding (treating \(x_i\) as constant, \(\mu\) as the variable):

\[ Q_i = x_i'Bx_i - x_i'B\mu - \mu'Bx_i + \mu'B\mu \]

Because \(\Sigma\) (and hence \(B=\Sigma^{-1}\)) is a covariance matrix, it's symmetric: \(B'=B\). This means \(x_i'B\mu\) and \(\mu'Bx_i\) are actually equal (each is the transpose of the other, and both are scalars, so a scalar equals its own transpose). So the middle two terms combine:

\[ Q_i = x_i'Bx_i - 2\mu'Bx_i + \mu'B\mu \]

Step 2 — differentiate

Using the standard matrix-calculus identities \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mu}(a'\mu)=a\) and \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mu}(\mu'B\mu)=2B\mu\) (when \(B\) is symmetric), and noting the \(x_i'Bx_i\) term doesn't involve \(\mu\) at all:

\[ \frac{\partial Q_i}{\partial\mu} = 0 - 2Bx_i + 2B\mu = -2B(x_i-\mu) = -2\Sigma^{-1}(x_i-\mu) \]

Step 3 — sum over all observations and solve

\[ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mu} = -\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial Q_i}{\partial\mu} = -\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\big[-2\Sigma^{-1}(x_i-\mu)\big] = \Sigma^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\mu) \]

Setting this to zero, and since \(\Sigma^{-1}\) is non-singular (so we can "cancel" it):

\[ \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\mu)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_{i=1}^n x_i - n\mu = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat\mu = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n x_i = \bar X \]

Key takeaway

\[ \boxed{\hat\mu = \bar X} \]

The maximum likelihood estimator of the multivariate mean vector is exactly the sample mean vector — the direct multivariate analogue of the univariate MLE result \(\hat\mu=\bar x\).

7. MLE of the covariance matrix

Now we keep \(\mu\) fixed at its just-found MLE, \(\hat\mu=\bar X\), and differentiate \(\ell\) with respect to \(\Sigma\) instead.

Step 1 — rewrite the quadratic-form sum as a trace

Notice \((x_i-\mu)'\Sigma^{-1}(x_i-\mu)\) is a scalar (a \(1\times1\) matrix) — you can check the dimensions: \((1\times p)(p\times p)(p\times1) = 1\times1\). Your notes use a classic trick: any scalar equals its own trace, and the trace has a "cyclic property" allowing us to reorder a product inside it:

Matrix result used

For a \(k\times k\) symmetric matrix \(A\) and a \(k\times1\) vector \(x\): \(x'Ax = \text{tr}(x'Ax) = \text{tr}(Axx')\) (cyclic property of trace).

Applying this to our sum:

\[ (x_i-\mu)'\Sigma^{-1}(x_i-\mu) = \text{tr}\big[\Sigma^{-1}(x_i-\mu)(x_i-\mu)'\big] \]

Summing over \(i\), and defining \(A=\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\mu)(x_i-\mu)'\) (our old friend, the cross-product matrix from Part 3, Section 2!):

\[ \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\mu)'\Sigma^{-1}(x_i-\mu) = \text{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}A) \]

So the log-likelihood simplifies beautifully to:

\[ \ell = \text{constant} - \frac{n}{2}\log|\Sigma| - \frac12\text{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}A) \]

Step 2 — differentiate with respect to Σ

We need two matrix-calculus building blocks: \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\Sigma}\log|\Sigma|\) and \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\Sigma}\text{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}A)\). Your notes derive both carefully in an appendix — full derivations are in the expandable box below if you want the complete rigor, but here are the results:

Results (proofs below)

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial\Sigma}\log|\Sigma| = \Sigma^{-1} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial\Sigma}\text{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}A) = -\Sigma^{-1}A\Sigma^{-1} \]

▸ Show the proof of both matrix-calculus identities

Identity 1: \(\partial\log|\Sigma|/\partial\Sigma=\Sigma^{-1}\). By the chain rule, \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\Sigma}\log|\Sigma| = \dfrac{1}{|\Sigma|}\cdot\dfrac{\partial|\Sigma|}{\partial\Sigma}\). Jacobi's formula gives the derivative of a matrix determinant with respect to the matrix itself: \(\dfrac{\partial|\Sigma|}{\partial\Sigma} = \text{adj}(\Sigma)^\top = \text{adj}(\Sigma)\) (since \(\Sigma\) is symmetric). By the definition of a matrix inverse, \(\Sigma^{-1}=\dfrac{\text{adj}(\Sigma)}{|\Sigma|}\), i.e. \(\text{adj}(\Sigma)=|\Sigma|\Sigma^{-1}\). Substituting: \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\Sigma}\log|\Sigma| = \dfrac{1}{|\Sigma|}\cdot|\Sigma|\Sigma^{-1} = \Sigma^{-1}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Identity 2: \(\partial\,\text{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}A)/\partial\Sigma = -\Sigma^{-1}A\Sigma^{-1}\). We use the differential calculus of matrices. A standard identity for the differential of an inverse: \(d(\Sigma^{-1}) = -\Sigma^{-1}(d\Sigma)\Sigma^{-1}\). So:

\[ d\big[\text{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}A)\big] = \text{tr}\big[d(\Sigma^{-1})A\big] = \text{tr}\big[-\Sigma^{-1}(d\Sigma)\Sigma^{-1}A\big] = \text{tr}\big[-\Sigma^{-1}A\Sigma^{-1}\,d\Sigma\big] \]

(the last step uses the cyclic property of trace to move \(d\Sigma\) to the end). For a general function \(f(\Sigma)\), the differential is given by \(df = \text{tr}\!\left[\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial\Sigma}\right)^\top d\Sigma\right]\). Matching this against what we derived:

\[ \left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\Sigma}\text{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}A)\right]^\top = -\Sigma^{-1}A\Sigma^{-1} \]

and since \(\Sigma\) and \(A\) are both symmetric, \(-\Sigma^{-1}A\Sigma^{-1}\) is itself symmetric, so transposing changes nothing: \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\Sigma}\text{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}A) = -\Sigma^{-1}A\Sigma^{-1}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Step 3 — set the derivative to zero and solve

\[ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\Sigma} = -\frac{n}{2}\Sigma^{-1} - \frac12\big[-\Sigma^{-1}A\Sigma^{-1}\big] = -\frac{n}{2}\Sigma^{-1}+\frac12\Sigma^{-1}A\Sigma^{-1} = 0 \]

Multiply through by \(-2\): \(\ n\Sigma^{-1} - \Sigma^{-1}A\Sigma^{-1} = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ \Sigma^{-1}A\Sigma^{-1}=n\Sigma^{-1}\).

Multiply both sides on the left by \(\Sigma\): \(\ A\Sigma^{-1}=nI\). Then multiply both sides on the right by \(\Sigma\): \(\ A = n\Sigma\).

\[ \Longrightarrow \quad \hat\Sigma = \frac{A}{n} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\hat\mu)(x_i-\hat\mu)' = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar X)(x_i-\bar X)' \]

Key takeaway

\[ \boxed{\hat\Sigma_{MLE} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar X)(x_i-\bar X)' = \frac{(n-1)}{n}S} \]

where \(S\) is the usual (unbiased) sample covariance matrix from Part 1. Notice the MLE divides by \(n\), while the "usual" sample covariance matrix \(S\) divides by \(n-1\) — exactly mirroring the univariate fact that the MLE of \(\sigma^2\) is biased (divides by \(n\)), while the standard unbiased sample variance divides by \(n-1\). The two estimators differ only by the small factor \(\frac{n-1}{n}\), which shrinks toward 1 as the sample size grows.

Likelihood Ratio Tests & Confidence Regions

Every hypothesis test so far in this guide — the \(t\)-test, Hotelling's \(T^2\) — was built for one specific job. This part introduces a completely general recipe, the Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT), that can test almost any hypothesis about almost any parameter, and then uses that same idea to build confidence regions — the multivariate generalisation of a confidence interval.

Page 1

1. The idea behind the Likelihood Ratio Test

Until now, in hypothesis testing, we have calculated a statistic — \(t\), \(F\), or Hotelling's \(T^2\). Each one was purpose-built for a specific kind of hypothesis. Today's approach is different: a single, more general recipe called the Likelihood Ratio Test.

Definition — the setup

Under the null hypothesis: \(H_0:\theta\in\Theta_0\). Under the full model: \(\theta\in\Theta\), where \(\Theta_0\subseteq\Theta\).

The null hypothesis puts restrictions on the possible values of the parameters. The alternative allows more freedom. The likelihood ratio test compares these two situations.

Worked example from your notes

Suppose we have a normal distribution. Under the full model, \(\mu\) can be any value — here, \(\mu>0\) (the natural range for the quantity being modelled). But under \(H_0:\mu=10\), we impose a restriction: \(\mu\) must be 10.

Which model should fit the data better: the unrestricted model, where \(\mu\) can take any (positive) value, or the restricted model, where we force \(\mu=10\)?

Your notes are cut off by the bottom of the page right at the answer to this question — but the very next page picks the thread back up and answers it directly: the unrestricted model will always fit at least as well, because it has strictly more freedom to chase whatever the data actually looks like. That's the key idea the rest of this page and the next one build on.

Maximizing the likelihood, with and without the restriction

Under the full model, maximize \(L(\theta)\) over all possible parameter values:

\[ \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} L(\theta) \]

Under the null hypothesis, maximize the likelihood subject to the restriction in \(H_0\):

\[ \sup_{\theta\in\Theta_0} L(\theta) \]

Now compare them.

Definition — the Likelihood Ratio

\[ \Lambda = \frac{\text{Maximum likelihood under } H_0}{\text{Maximum likelihood under the full model}} = \frac{\text{restricted}}{\text{unrestricted}} = \frac{\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_0}L(\theta)}{\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}L(\theta)} \]

Because the unrestricted model has strictly more freedom, its best-possible likelihood can never be smaller than the restricted model's:

\[ \sup_{\theta\in\Theta_0}L(\theta) \;\le\; \sup_{\theta\in\Theta}L(\theta) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{0\le\Lambda\le1} \]

Reading the value of Λ
  1. If \(H_0\) is true, or close to true, the restriction doesn't hurt the fit very much: \(L_0\approx L_{full}\Rightarrow\Lambda\approx1\). So we do not reject \(H_0\).
  2. If \(H_0\) is not supported by the data, forcing the restriction makes the likelihood much smaller: \(L_0\ll L_{full}\Rightarrow\Lambda\approx0\). So we have evidence against \(H_0\).

"The smaller the likelihood ratio, the stronger the evidence against \(H_0\)."

Page 3

2. Why we study the LRT in Multivariate Analysis

Many multivariate hypotheses impose restrictions on covariance matrices, mean vectors, correlations, and so on. Hotelling's \(T^2\) was designed mainly for testing mean vectors, but the LRT gives us a much more general framework — the same recipe covers all of these:

\[ H_0: \mu=\mu_0 \qquad H_0: \Sigma=\Sigma_0 \qquad\Big\}\ \text{restrictions} \]

or equality of covariance matrices across populations, and many others besides.

Setup for the derivation

Let \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\) be a random sample of size \(n\) from a \(p\)-variate normal distribution \(N_p(\mu,\Sigma)\). The likelihood function is:

\[ L(\mu,\Sigma) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{np/2}|\Sigma|^{n/2}}\exp\left[-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\mu)'\Sigma^{-1}(X_i-\mu)\right] \]

Under the full parameter space \(\Theta=\{(\mu,\Sigma)\}\), the MLEs are — from Part 3 — already known:

\[ \hat\mu = \bar X = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i, \qquad \hat\Sigma = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)(X_i-\bar X)' \qquad \text{—— (1)} \]

3. Deriving the LRT statistic for testing a mean vector

To build \(\Lambda\), we need the maximum likelihood value in both the full model and the restricted model. Simplifying the exponent of the likelihood uses the same "trace trick" already introduced in Part 3.

Recall — the trace trick

Let \(A\) be a \(k\times k\) symmetric matrix and \(x\) be a \((k\times1)\) vector. Then:

\[ x'Ax = \text{tr}(x'Ax) = \text{tr}(Axx') \]

Simplifying the unrestricted (full-model) exponent

Using this result in the exponent term of the MND, we get:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)'\hat\Sigma^{-1}(X_i-\bar X) = \sum_{i=1}^n\text{tr}\big(\hat\Sigma^{-1}(X_i-\bar X)(X_i-\bar X)'\big) = \text{tr}\left[\hat\Sigma^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)(X_i-\bar X)'\right] \]

Since \(\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)(X_i-\bar X)' = n\hat\Sigma\) (using equation (1)):

\[ = \text{tr}\big[\hat\Sigma^{-1}\cdot n\hat\Sigma\big] = \text{tr}[n\cdot I_p] = n\cdot\text{tr}(I_p) = np \]

Substituting this back into the likelihood function gives the unrestricted maximum likelihood:

\[ \max_{\mu,\Sigma}L(\mu,\Sigma) = L(\hat\mu,\hat\Sigma) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{np/2}|\hat\Sigma|^{n/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-np}2\right) \qquad\text{—— (2)} \]

Restricted maximization under H₀

We test \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\) vs. \(H_1:\mu\ne\mu_0\). Under \(H_0\), \(\mu\) is fixed at \(\mu_0\) — it's no longer free to be estimated. The likelihood becomes:

\[ L(\mu_0,\Sigma) = \frac1{(2\pi)^{np/2}|\Sigma|^{n/2}}\exp\left[-\frac12\sum_j(X_j-\mu_0)'\Sigma^{-1}(X_j-\mu_0)\right] \]

Under \(H_0\), the MLE for \(\Sigma\) is now built around \(\mu_0\) instead of \(\bar X\):

\[ \hat\Sigma_0 = \frac1n\sum_j(X_j-\mu_0)(X_j-\mu_0)' \]

Applying the exact same trace trick with \(\hat\Sigma_0\) gives the exponent \(=np\) again, so the restricted maximum likelihood is:

\[ \max_{\Sigma}L(\mu_0,\Sigma) = L(\mu_0,\hat\Sigma_0) = \frac1{(2\pi)^{np/2}|\hat\Sigma_0|^{n/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-np}2\right) \qquad\text{—— (3)} \]

Constructing the likelihood ratio statistic

\[ \Lambda = \frac{\max_\Sigma L(\mu_0,\Sigma)}{\max_{\mu,\Sigma}L(\mu,\Sigma)} = \frac{L(\mu_0,\hat\Sigma_0)}{L(\hat\mu,\hat\Sigma)} \]

Substituting equations (2) and (3) — the \((2\pi)^{np/2}\) terms and the \(\exp(-np/2)\) terms are identical top and bottom, and cancel completely:

\[ \Lambda = \frac{\dfrac1{(2\pi)^{np/2}|\hat\Sigma_0|^{n/2}}\exp\left(\dfrac{-np}2\right)}{\dfrac1{(2\pi)^{np/2}|\hat\Sigma|^{n/2}}\exp\left(\dfrac{-np}2\right)} = \frac{|\hat\Sigma|^{n/2}}{|\hat\Sigma_0|^{n/2}} = \left(\frac{|\hat\Sigma|}{|\hat\Sigma_0|}\right)^{n/2} \]

Wilk's Lambda

The equivalent statistic

\[ \Lambda^{2/n} = \frac{|\hat\Sigma|}{|\hat\Sigma_0|} \]

is called Wilk's Lambda. If the observed value of the likelihood ratio is too small, \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\) is rejected.

Why this matters

Notice what \(\hat\Sigma\) and \(\hat\Sigma_0\) actually measure: \(\hat\Sigma\) is built around the sample's own best-fitting centre \(\bar X\), so it captures the smallest possible spread the data could have. \(\hat\Sigma_0\) is built around the hypothesised (possibly wrong) centre \(\mu_0\) instead, so if \(\mu_0\) is far from where the data actually sits, \(\hat\Sigma_0\) will come out inflated — larger in every direction — to "explain away" that extra distance. A large \(|\hat\Sigma_0|\) relative to \(|\hat\Sigma|\) is exactly what makes \(\Lambda\) small, and that's precisely the signal that \(\mu_0\) is a poor guess for the true mean.

4. Confidence intervals — the univariate case, revisited

So far, we have studied estimation and hypothesis testing — we've estimated unknown parameters, and we've tested hypotheses about them. Now we discuss another important concept: confidence intervals and confidence regions.

Definition

"A confidence interval gives us a range of plausible values for an unknown population parameter."

Suppose \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\). We want to estimate \(\mu\). From the sample, we calculate \(\bar X\). Does one value, \(\bar X\), tell us exactly where \(\mu\) is? No. So instead we give a range of plausible values for \(\mu\) — a confidence interval.

Worked example from your notes

Suppose we want to estimate the average height of students in a college. We take a random sample of \(n=10\) students and obtain \(\bar X=170\text{cm}\), \(S=6\text{cm}\) (\(\sigma\) unknown). We know the sample mean is 170cm — but can we conclude the population mean is exactly 170cm? No — the sample mean will change from sample to sample. So instead of giving one estimate, we want a range of plausible values for the true population mean \(\mu\).

This is exactly where the \(t\)-statistic comes back in:

\[ t = \frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n} \sim t_{n-1} \]

General \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval:

\[ P\left[-t_{\alpha/2,n-1} \le \frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n} \le t_{\alpha/2,n-1}\right] = 1-\alpha \]

Rearranging (multiply through by \(S/\sqrt n\), then add \(\bar X\) to every term), we obtain:

\[ \bar X - t_{\alpha/2,n-1}\frac{S}{\sqrt n} \;\le\; \mu \;\le\; \bar X + t_{\alpha/2,n-1}\frac{S}{\sqrt n} \]

Or more compactly — the confidence interval:

\[ \boxed{\bar X \pm t_{\alpha/2,n-1}\frac{S}{\sqrt n}} \]

\((1-\alpha)\) represents the probability in the central region — the "confidence level." The small areas outside \(\pm t_{\alpha/2}\) are the values that our confidence procedure does not cover.

Worked example — 95% confidence

\(\alpha=0.05 \Rightarrow 1-\alpha = 1-0.05 = 0.95 \Rightarrow 100(1-\alpha)\% = 95\%\).

The correct interpretation — worth memorising word for word

"If we repeatedly take many random samples and construct a 95% confidence interval using the same method, approximately 95 out of every 100 intervals will contain the true population mean." Equivalently: 95% of confidence intervals (out of 100) contain \(\mu\); approximately 5 confidence intervals out of 100 miss \(\mu\). This is a statement about the procedure, repeated many times — not a probability statement about any single interval you've already computed.

5. From a confidence interval to a confidence region

So far we have learned only one variable — suppose we want to estimate the population mean \(\mu\):

\[ \bar X - t_{\alpha/2,n-1}\frac{S}{\sqrt n} \le \mu \le \bar X + t_{\alpha/2,n-1}\frac{S}{\sqrt n} \]

Here \(\mu\) is only one number. Therefore all possible values of \(\mu\) lie on a number line.

What happens when we have more than one variable?

\[ X_i = \begin{bmatrix}X_{i1}\\X_{i2}\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mu = \begin{bmatrix}\mu_1\\\mu_2\end{bmatrix} \]

In multivariate analysis, the variables may be correlated. Therefore, we want one joint region that covers all variables together — not two separate, independent intervals.

Dimension of parameterWhat we build
One-dimensional parameterConfidence Interval
\(p\)-dimensional parameter spaceConfidence Region

Recall the univariate \(t\)-statistic, squared:

\[ t = \frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}, \qquad t^2 = \frac{n(\bar X-\mu)^2}{S^2} \]

This measures how far the unknown population mean \(\mu\) is from the sample \(\bar X\), after accounting for variability. Now generalise it to vectors — this is exactly Hotelling's \(T^2\) from Part 1:

\[ T^2 = n(\bar X-\mu)'S^{-1}(\bar X-\mu) \]

The key analogy

This is exactly the same idea as the univariate \(t^2\), but now we are measuring the distance between two vectors instead of two numbers — using the Mahalanobis distance from Part 1 rather than ordinary squared distance. In the univariate case, all values of \(\mu\) that fall within our acceptable \(t\)-range form the confidence interval. In exactly the same way, in multivariate analysis, all mean vectors \(\mu\) that fall within the acceptable Hotelling's \(T^2\) range form the confidence region.

6. The confidence region formula, for a one-sample multivariate normal population

Recall from Part 1 the sampling distribution of one-sample Hotelling's \(T^2\):

\[ \frac{(n-p)}{p(n-1)}T^2 \sim F_{p,n-p} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad T^2 \sim \frac{(n-1)p}{n-p}F_{p,n-p} \]

Therefore, we do not reject the proposed \(\mu\) when:

\[ T^2 \le \frac{p(n-1)}{n-p}F_{p,n-p}(\alpha) \]

Hence, the set of all such \(\mu\) is:

Key result — the confidence region for a mean vector

\[ \boxed{n(\bar X-\mu)'S^{-1}(\bar X-\mu) \;\le\; \frac{p(n-1)}{n-p}F_{p,n-p}(\alpha)} \]

and this is the \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence region for the population mean vector.

What this region actually looks like

For \(p=2\), this inequality traces out the inside of an ellipse centred at \(\bar X\) — not a rectangle formed from two separate intervals. The shape and tilt of that ellipse are governed entirely by \(S^{-1}\): if the two variables are strongly correlated, the ellipse tilts and stretches along that correlation direction, exactly reflecting the fact that some combinations of \((\mu_1,\mu_2)\) are far more plausible, jointly, than others — information two separate one-at-a-time confidence intervals would throw away.

7. Worked example — a confidence region for the difference between two mean vectors

The one-sample confidence region generalises immediately to the two-sample case, using exactly the two-sample Hotelling's \(T^2\) machinery from Part 1, Section 10. Let \(\delta=\mu_A-\mu_B\) be the true (unknown) difference between two population mean vectors. Following the identical logic as the one-sample case:

Key result — confidence region for a difference of mean vectors

\[ \frac{n_An_B}{n_A+n_B}\big[(\bar X_A-\bar X_B)-\delta\big]'S_p^{-1}\big[(\bar X_A-\bar X_B)-\delta\big] \;\le\; \frac{(n_A+n_B-2)p}{n_A+n_B-p-1}F_{p,\,n_A+n_B-p-1}(\alpha) \]

Worked example, from your notes

Age and risky behaviour are two important factors that make a difference between the accident group (AG) and non-accident group (NAG) of workers. Random samples of 20 individuals from AG and 50 individuals from NAG were collected. The sample mean vector and sample covariance matrix are given. Construct a 95% confidence region for the difference between the two population mean vectors.

Sample 1 (AG)Sample 2 (NAG)
Mean vector\(\bar X_A=\begin{bmatrix}50\\6\end{bmatrix}\)\(\bar X_B=\begin{bmatrix}40\\8\end{bmatrix}\)
Covariance matrix\(S_A=\begin{bmatrix}16&-5\\-5&4\end{bmatrix}\)\(S_B=\begin{bmatrix}25&-6\\-6&9\end{bmatrix}\)

Solution. \(n_A=20\), \(n_B=50\), \(p=2\). We want to construct a 95% confidence region for \(\delta=\mu_A-\mu_B=\begin{bmatrix}\delta_1\\\delta_2\end{bmatrix}\).

First, pool the two covariance matrices:

\[ S_p = \frac{(n_A-1)S_A+(n_B-1)S_B}{n_A+n_B-2} = \frac{19\begin{bmatrix}16&-5\\-5&4\end{bmatrix}+49\begin{bmatrix}25&-6\\-6&9\end{bmatrix}}{68} \]

\[ S_p = \begin{bmatrix}22.4853 & -5.7206\\-5.7206 & 7.6029\end{bmatrix} \]

The two-sample \(T^2\) statistic (from Part 1, Section 10) is:

\[ T^2 = \frac{n_An_B}{n_A+n_B}\big[(\bar X_A-\bar X_B)-\delta\big]'S_p^{-1}\big[(\bar X_A-\bar X_B)-\delta\big] \]

For a 95% CR, we need the critical value:

\[ T^2 \le \frac{(n_A+n_B-2)p}{n_A+n_B-p-1}F_{p,\,n_A+n_B-p-1}(\alpha) = \frac{68\times2}{67}F_{2,67}(0.05) = \frac{136}{67}\times3.134 \approx \boxed{6.361} \]

Your notes leave the final step as an exercise — "Solve it." Here it is, completed.

The difference in sample means is \(\bar X_A-\bar X_B = \begin{bmatrix}50-40\\6-8\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}10\\-2\end{bmatrix}\), and inverting \(S_p\):

\[ S_p^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix}0.0550 & 0.0414\\0.0414 & 0.1627\end{bmatrix} \]

The confidence region is every \((\delta_1,\delta_2)\) satisfying:

\[ \frac{n_An_B}{n_A+n_B}\begin{bmatrix}10-\delta_1 & -2-\delta_2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0.0550 & 0.0414\\0.0414 & 0.1627\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}10-\delta_1\\-2-\delta_2\end{bmatrix} \;\le\; 6.361 \]

With \(\dfrac{n_An_B}{n_A+n_B}=\dfrac{20\times50}{70}=14.286\), divide both sides through by this constant to isolate the quadratic form:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}10-\delta_1 & -2-\delta_2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0.0550 & 0.0414\\0.0414 & 0.1627\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}10-\delta_1\\-2-\delta_2\end{bmatrix} \;\le\; \frac{6.361}{14.286} \approx 0.445 \]

Multiplying out the quadratic form explicitly:

\[ 0.0550(10-\delta_1)^2 \;+\; 0.0828(10-\delta_1)(-2-\delta_2) \;+\; 0.1627(-2-\delta_2)^2 \;\le\; 0.445 \]

This is the completed 95% confidence region — an ellipse in the \((\delta_1,\delta_2)\)-plane, centred at \((\delta_1,\delta_2)=(10,-2)\) (the observed difference in sample means), tilted and stretched according to \(S_p^{-1}\). Any hypothesised difference \(\delta=(\delta_1,\delta_2)\) that satisfies this inequality is a plausible value for the true difference between the accident-group and non-accident-group mean vectors, at 95% confidence; any \(\delta\) outside it is rejected.

A quick sanity check worth doing on any confidence region

Plug in \(\delta=(10,-2)\) — the centre of the region, exactly the observed \(\bar X_A-\bar X_B\) — and both bracketed terms vanish, giving \(0\le0.445\). ✓ The centre point always trivially satisfies its own confidence region; this is a useful one-line check that the inequality's direction and the sign of every term have been set up correctly, before trusting the region for anything further.

What Part 4 adds to your toolkit

The Likelihood Ratio Test is the single most general recipe in this guide — restrict the parameter space under \(H_0\), compare the best-possible fit under the restriction against the best-possible fit without it, and a small ratio is evidence against \(H_0\). Applied to a multivariate mean vector, it reproduces exactly the \(|\hat\Sigma|/|\hat\Sigma_0|\) statistic known as Wilk's Lambda. And the same "how far is my estimate from a candidate parameter, measured in Mahalanobis units" idea that built Hotelling's \(T^2\) in Part 1 also builds confidence regions — the direct multivariate analogue of a confidence interval, tracing out an ellipse (or higher-dimensional ellipsoid) of jointly plausible parameter values, rather than a separate interval per variable.

Mini glossary

Mean vector \(\mu\)/\(\bar X\)The stacked average of every variable — population/sample version respectively.
Covariance matrix \(\Sigma\)/\(S\)Diagonal = each variable's own variance; off-diagonal = covariance between each pair of variables.
Mahalanobis distance\((x-\mu)'\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\) — "distance from the mean," corrected for the shape/correlation of the data's spread.
Partial correlation \(r_{12.3}\)Correlation between \(X_1,X_2\) after removing the linear effect of \(X_3\). Can be negative.
Multiple correlation \(R_{1.23}\)How strongly \(X_1\) is related to its best joint linear prediction from \(X_2,X_3\). Never negative.
Wishart distribution \(W_p(n,\Sigma)\)The distribution of a sum-of-cross-products matrix built from \(n\) independent \(N_p(0,\Sigma)\) vectors — the matrix version of Chi-square.
Hotelling's \(T^2\)The multivariate generalisation of the (squared) t-statistic, used to test hypotheses about mean vectors.
MLEThe parameter values that make the observed data "most likely" under the assumed model.
Likelihood Ratio \(\Lambda\)Restricted (under \(H_0\)) maximum likelihood, divided by unrestricted (full-model) maximum likelihood. Always between 0 and 1; small values are evidence against \(H_0\).
Wilk's Lambda\(\Lambda^{2/n}=|\hat\Sigma|/|\hat\Sigma_0|\) — the LRT statistic for testing a mean vector, comparing the sample covariance built around \(\bar X\) against the covariance built around the hypothesised \(\mu_0\).
Confidence regionThe multivariate generalisation of a confidence interval — a region of jointly plausible parameter vectors, built from Hotelling's \(T^2\) and traced out as an ellipse (or ellipsoid) rather than a rectangle of separate intervals.
